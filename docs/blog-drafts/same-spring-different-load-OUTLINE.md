# Outline: same spring, different load

## Thesis (one sentence)

Two clients on the same spring setting are not doing the same exercise, because
the load at the joint comes from the spring and from the client's own body, and
how heavy and long their limbs are can change both the amount of work and the
direction of it.

## 1. Open: the bridge that could only clench

## 2. What "load" means here

- Define load once and keep it: how hard the work actually is for that body.
- The spring is one source. The body is the other. Legs, head, arms all weigh
  something, and they're attached to joints that have to hold them.
- One line on torque: a force that rotates a joint. How much depends on how big
  the force is and how far from the joint it acts (the moment arm).
- τ = F × d. We don't do math while teaching. The equation is useful because it
  names the two dials: how heavy, and how far out.

## 3. Example 1: Teaser (the body is the whole load)

No spring in the picture, which makes it the cleanest place to start.

- Same height, different weight: same moment arm, heavier legs. About 92% more
  torque at the hip.
- Same weight, different height: same leg weight, longer moment arm. About 16%
  more.
- Lesson: weight changes the load a lot, height changes it less, and neither one
  is about strength or effort.

## 4. Example 2: Feet in Straps (the sign flips)

The centerpiece. Teaser shows a different amount of load. This shows a
different kind of work.

- Two clients, both 5'9", 150 lb and 300 lb, Balanced Body red + blue.
- 150 lb: +89 lb-in. The springs win. They have to press their legs down.
- 300 lb: -260 lb-in. The legs win. They have to hold their legs up.
- Same springs, same exercise name, opposite job for the muscles.
- Tie-in: the springs are supporting one client and resisting the other. Link

## 5. Example 3: supine arms in straps (you can move the dial)

The turn from "bodies differ" to "here's what you control."

- Same springs, same arm movement. Load on the core changes by moving the head
  and legs farther from the pivot.
- Layer 1 head down, tabletop. Layer 2 add the curl. Layer 3 extend one leg.
  Layer 4 extend both.
- How I run it in a group class: everyone starts on the same layer, and every
  couple of reps I invite them to advance if their body is up for it today.
- Point: in a group class, the moment arm is a dial you can hand out per person
  without touching a single spring.

## 6. So what do you do with this?

- Don't do the math for every client. They can tell you what they feel.
- Know your intent first. Do you want them pressing down or holding up?
  Articulating or working hard? Then pick the setting for that body.
- The spring is the loudest dial, not the only one: springs, rope length, leg
  angle, head up or down, which layer.
- The "Am I the problem?" beat, kept short and pointed at the habit, not the
  reader: when a client doesn't feel what you're describing, it's usually not
  them. The load was never right for their body. You're the person in the room
  who can fix that.

## Physics notes (fix before drafting)

1. **The Teaser reel and the Feet in Straps carousel used different body
   models.** The reel's leg weight works out to about 21% of body weight and
   its moment arm implies a leg center of mass closer to the hip. The carousel
   states 18% per leg, leg length 49% of height, center of mass 43% down the
   leg. For one post they should match. I recomputed Teaser with the carousel's
   assumptions (table in section 3). The percentage differences barely move
   (92% stays 92%, 15% becomes 16%) but the absolute numbers change, so the
   blog numbers won't match the reel. I'd state the assumptions once in a small
   note or footnote.
2. **Per leg vs. both legs.** I checked the Feet in Straps numbers and they
   hold together as per-leg values (one leg against half the spring force,
   about 31 lb along the rope). The post should say "per leg" so nobody
   doubles it in their head. Teaser lifts both legs, so either say per leg
   there too or double those numbers.
3. **Units.** The reel used ft-lb and the carousel used lb-in with Nm. Pick
   one. I used lb-in with Nm in brackets to match the carousel.
4. **Supine arms layer 4.** Use the corrected wording: the moment arm doesn't
   double, the load on the long moment arm does.
5. **The bridge story's physics.** Keep the claim small: for that body, the
   combined load was more than the skill could happen under, and the extra
   spring took some of it away. Don't try to put numbers on it.
