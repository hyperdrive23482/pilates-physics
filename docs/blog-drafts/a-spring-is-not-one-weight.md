When a new client tells me they did Pilates somewhere else and "used a red spring," I always smile a little. Because I know something they do not know yet: a red spring is not a thing. Not really.

We talk about springs the way we talk about dumbbells. A 10 pound dumbbell is 10 pounds whether you hold it at your hip or press it overhead. We assume a spring works the same way, that "two reds and a blue" is a fixed amount of resistance you can write down and carry from studio to studio. It is not. A spring is not one weight. It gets heavier the more you stretch it, and every manufacturer builds theirs a little differently.

So let's actually compare them. I charted every spring from six common reformer brands and lined them up against each other. What came out is the best argument I know for why understanding the physics matters more than memorizing a setting.

### A spring is a slope, not a number

Here is the one idea that changes how you read every spring chart: a spring's resistance is not a single value. It is a line that climbs as the spring stretches.

The model is simple:

**Force = (k × stretch) + b**

- **b** is the starting tension, the load the spring already pulls before the carriage moves.
- **k** is the rate, how fast the load climbs for every inch the carriage travels out.
- **stretch** is how far the spring is pulled, which depends on the movement and the machine.

![A Gratz single-spring force curve beside Balanced Body's four spring curves, every line sloping upward](IMG_GRATZ_BB)
*Left: Gratz makes a single spring. Right: Balanced Body makes four. Every line slopes up, which is the whole point.*

Look at Gratz first, because it makes the cleanest case. Gratz sells one spring. One. On paper that sounds like one weight. But the line climbs from about 6 pounds when it first engages to more than 50 pounds at full stretch. Same spring, same rep, roughly eight times heavier from the start of the carriage's travel to the end.

Balanced Body splits that range into four springs, but each one does the same thing: it slopes up. A Balanced Body red runs from about 8 pounds to about 44 pounds across the stroke. So when someone says "I was on a red," the honest answer to "how heavy was that" is "somewhere between 8 and 44 pounds, depending on where in the movement you ask." The spring setting is the start of the question, not the answer.

### Every brand draws its own lines

Now the part that surprises even experienced teachers. Here is how the six brands order their springs from lightest to heaviest.

| Brand | Lightest to heaviest |
|---|---|
| Balanced Body | Yellow (very light), Blue (light), Red (medium), Green (heavy) |
| Merrithew | White (25%), Blue (50%), Red (100%), Black (125%) |
| Align Pilates | Yellow (extra light), Blue (light), Red (medium), Green (strong) |
| Peak Pilates | Green (extra light), Blue (light), Yellow (medium), Red (heavy) |
| BASI | Yellow, Blue, Red (three springs only) |
| Gratz | One standard spring |

Read it twice. **Green** is the heaviest spring Balanced Body and Align make, and the lightest spring Peak makes. **Yellow** is the lightest spring on a Balanced Body and a middle spring on a Peak. **Red** is a medium for Balanced Body, Align, and Merrithew, but a heavy for Peak and BASI. The colors are not a language. They are just paint.

It runs deeper than color. Look at the actual curves.

![Peak Pilates spring curves beside BASI spring curves](IMG_PEAK_BASI)
*Peak and BASI. Notice how high every Peak line starts, and that BASI makes only three springs.*

![Merrithew spring curves labeled by percentage beside Align Pilates spring curves](IMG_MERRITHEW_ALIGN)
*Merrithew labels by percentage. Align's strong green climbs steeper than almost anything else here.*

Two design choices separate these brands more than anything else: how much **starting tension** they build in, and how **steeply** the load climbs.

Peak is the high-starting-tension brand. A Peak red already pulls about 17 pounds at the home position, while a Balanced Body red pulls about 8. Peak more than doubles the starting load before the carriage moves an inch. But Peak's lines climb gently from there.

Align is the opposite. Its springs start light, some nearly at zero, then ramp up hard. Its strong green climbs so steeply it finishes higher than any other spring in this whole comparison.

The rest sit in between, each with its own mix. BASI makes only three springs, so its heaviest tops out around what other brands call a medium. Merrithew skips weight names and labels by percentage of a baseline. Even the tier names disagree: what Balanced Body calls "very light," Peak and Align call "extra light," and what Balanced Body calls "heavy," Align calls "strong."

### The same "weight," brand by brand

So what happens when you put the matching tier from every brand on one graph? The lines fan out, and then they start crossing.

![Cross-brand comparison of the lightest springs beside the next tier up](IMG_COMPARE_LIGHT_MED)
*Lightest springs (left) and the next tier up (right). At the light end the brands run close together. One tier heavier and they already spread and begin to cross.*

![Cross-brand comparison of the two heaviest tiers of springs](IMG_COMPARE_HEAVY_XH)
*The two heaviest tiers. This is where "equivalent" falls apart.*

Watch the extra-heavy graph on the right. Every spring on it is the heaviest its brand makes. At the start of the stroke, Peak's red is the heaviest of the group at about 17 pounds. By full extension, both Align and Merrithew have passed it, and only Balanced Body's green sits lower. Align's strong green does the opposite: it starts down in the pack near 10 pounds and finishes around 83, more than 20 pounds clear of Peak.

That is the thesis in one picture. You cannot rank two springs with a single number, because the ranking itself changes depending on how far the carriage has traveled. The spring that feels heaviest at the start of a movement can be the lightest at the end.

### About that conversion chart

People always want the cheat sheet. "What is a Balanced Body red on a Merrithew?" So here is a conversion chart that lines up roughly equivalent springs across brands.

![A color grid mapping each brand's springs into extra light, light, medium, and heavy columns](IMG_CONVERSION)
*A starting point, not a law of physics. Equivalence holds at one moment in the stretch and drifts everywhere else.*

Use it. It is genuinely helpful for getting in the ballpark when you switch equipment. But know what it is. This chart files four springs under "heavy": Balanced Body green, Align green, Peak red, and Merrithew black. We just watched those four range from about 52 to 83 pounds at full stretch and trade places along the way. They share a column. They are not the same spring.

A conversion chart picks one point in the stretch and says "these match there." Because every spring has its own starting tension and its own rate, springs that match in the middle will not match at the ends. The chart is a translator, not a guarantee. It gets you close. It does not make two springs equal.

### Why "the same springs" still won't feel the same

Here is the part I most want teachers to sit with, and the real reason I built all of this.

Even if you put the exact same springs on two different reformers, your clients will not feel the same thing.

Go back to the model: Force = (k × stretch) + b. Identical springs fix k and b. But force also depends on stretch, and stretch is set by the machine, not the spring. Carriage travel length, where the spring anchors to the frame, how far the carriage sits from the spring bar at home, rope length, pulley height: all of it changes how far a given movement actually stretches the spring. A classical Gratz and a contemporary reformer can run the same footwork and stretch the spring through different distances, landing you at different points on that climbing line. Same spring. Different load.

That is why you cannot teach from the spring setting alone. The setting is one input. Machine geometry is another. Rope length is another. Footbar and pulley height are others. If all you know is "two reds," you know one variable out of many and you are guessing at the rest. Understanding how each of those settings moves a body along the spring's force curve is the actual skill, and it is most of what the rest of this course is about.

### And your clients are not standardized either

One more layer, and this one should take the pressure off.

Even on one machine, with one spring, two clients will not feel the same load. A taller client and a shorter client press the carriage through different ranges. Different limb lengths change leverage. Different body weights change how much the spring helps or resists. The spring's curve is fixed. Where each body lives on that curve is not.

Which means two people on the very same Gratz, with the very same spring, are already feeling different things. The dream of dialing in one perfect universal setting, of making your studio match Gratz or anyone else so that everything transfers cleanly, was never really on the table. There is no setting that feels identical to every body, because no two bodies are identical.

That is not a problem to fix. It is the job. Our work was never to make the equipment uniform. It is to understand the system well enough to meet the specific body in front of us, on the specific machine we have, on the day they walk in.

A red spring is not a thing. But a teacher who understands why is worth a great deal.
