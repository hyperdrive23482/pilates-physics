I just got back from the [Pilates Journal Expo](https://www.pilatesjournalexpo.com/) in Miami and am buzzing from all the amazing conversations I had. It was awesome to meet so many of you IRL!

As you’d expect after a major industry event, I couldn’t stop thinking about what I heard on stage. The first business panel on Saturday, “[Pilates at Scale: Growth, Consolidation, and the next competitive era](https://www.pilatesjournalexpo.com/miami-schedule/pilates-scalegrowth-consolidation-next-competative-era)” featured CEOs from major brands talking about the future of Pilates. And the one thing they all agreed on was the importance of personalization.

But no one asked the follow up question: How?

**What Personalization in Pilates means today**

Look at any conference schedule and you’ll see a common theme among topics: get better at reading the body in front of you, and learn new ways to cue the movement to fix it.

Arguably, it’s the Pilates secret sauce. Small changes to how we move can drastically change the feel. It’s often an instantaneous change, and can be addicting. Help a client discover something new about their body and our instructor ego inflates and our clients feel enlightened.

But that’s not the only kind of personalization that we need to be considering. And I’m not talking about remembering a client’s birthday or asking how their kid’s basketball game went.

Growing the Pilates market means we need to adapt our secret sauce to the environment most of our clients are in and think beyond a single moment in a single session.

**The three prongs of Personalization in Pilates**

Pilates has three types of personalization. We’ve talked about the first: the observation/feedback cycle. The second and third are just as important.

Adapting to a group environment: Let’s be real, most Pilates clients are now experiencing the method in a group setting. It’s the most accessible form of Pilates for clients, and arguably the least ideally placed for utilizing our observation/feedback superpower. Managing a group class is a skill in itself that requires a deep understanding of how different client body types interact with the external loads (whether from their own body or the equipment) and concise language that enables personalization of that load to meet each client where they’re at.

We know a spring gets heavier the more it stretches. And if a client’s body size means they stretch their spring a different length than their neighbor, that means the load they experience is different.

We can agree, in principle, that the same spring is a different workout for each client. But instructors don’t have time to go around to each student in a 12-person class and give individual spring recommendations. The middle ground is having options you can offer quickly and simply to empower clients to find the setting that helps them feel your intent. 

Figuring out those options isn't guesswork, it's physics. That's what [Pilates Physics 101](https://pilatesphysics.com/pilates-physics-101) is for, and you can [run the numbers yourself](https://pilatesphysics.com/spring-calculator) if you want to see how big the difference between two bodies on the same spring really is.

Tracking progress over time: It’s [well-known and studied](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) that seeing progress over time is a motivator to stick with it. Other fitness methods have simple ways to do this. Running has heart rate and mile pace. Strength training has load, reps, sets. Pilates has… feeling.

This is tough for two reasons. First, in order to plan a great session you need to have a good history of past sessions with your client. Great teachers find a way to remember the bare minimum and wing it on the day of. For many people this is good enough. But for most of us, having a concrete base of objective information would help us plan or outline our next session faster and more accurately.

And second, we rely on our clients assessing how they feel to validate their participation in our sessions. Not to take away from the importance of a client feeling good, but “feeling” is a very intangible thing that is easily dependent on mood, stress, and other external factors that have nothing to do with the amazing Pilates session they just did. As we expand the reach of Pilates we need to find ways to reach the people who aren’t as connected to their bodies as we are, where feeling the change in their body is harder or less natural. And who knows, maybe our current clients will like the reinforcement, too.

**Technology can help us, it shouldn’t replace us**

During the same panel session talking about personalization, one panelist mentioned that we’re in the “people business.” This led to a general consensus that technology didn’t have a place in the studio.

Generally speaking, I was shocked. Not only do most studios run on a technology platform like [MindBody](https://www.mindbodyonline.com/), [Momence](https://www.momence.com/), [MarianaTek](https://www.marianatek.com/), or [Acuity](https://acuityscheduling.com/), but we were all together at a conference facilitated by technology! We can argue that two people sitting at a table scrolling Instagram disconnects them, but the flipside is that everyone at the conference can now stay connected with their new friends and mentors via instagram after we leave.

[Technology has a place in Pilates](https://pilatesphysics.com/blog/what-everyone-gets-wrong-about-tech-in-pilates). We as an industry picture it as a binary, scary thing. No, technology cannot and should not replace us as teachers and humans. But it can certainly augment our jobs so that we can do more of what we’re uniquely good at, and less of the stuff computers are good at. 

Humans are good at real-time connection, cueing, adjustment, and decision making in the Pilates space. I think computers are better at remembering things and doing repeatable, transactional tasks.

Adopting technology in that capacity will only help our unique talents shine brighter. It won’t dull the magic of Pilates.

That's the bet I made with [Remo](https://www.remopilates.com/). It listens while you teach and writes down what you actually did, so the history you need to plan next week's session is already there.  Over a run of sessions it turns into something you can hand your client: one red spring to two, double leg bridges to single leg, more articulation in a roll up.  It doesn't teach, and it doesn't decide what comes next. That's still you.

**What I want more of**

My weekend in Miami was awesome. I got to hear what industry leaders are thinking about the future of Pilates, meet lots of you, and move my body.

But it also left me feeling like the reality of what is happening in the studio today is not quite reflected in the broad conference content. We need more education on adapting for real bodies that are different from our training manuals, what it looks like to build the next session from the previous ones, and how to manage a group class environment while still delivering good Pilates.

A lot of that education is already happening online, through smart educators who are chipping away at making change webinar by webinar.

That work belongs on the main conference stage, too.
