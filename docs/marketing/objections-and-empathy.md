# Objections and Empathy

The two lists that sit under every piece of persuasion: what she pushes back
with, and what proves you already understand her. Plus the premise every
response lands on, and a repeatable pattern for answering any objection.

Written for **ICP 1, The Curious Practitioner**, unless a line is tagged
otherwise. See [icp-profiles.md](icp-profiles.md).

---

## The premise

Everything below rests on one claim. Say it three ways depending on the room.

**A. The sentence (default)**

> Pilates Physics teaches you to understand load, so you can adapt and progress
> any exercise for the body in front of you.

**B. The three beats (a rhythm, a sign-off, or a curriculum spine)**

> Understand the load. Adapt the exercise. Progress the client.

**C. The plain-language version, when "load" is a new word to the reader**

> Load is just how hard the work actually is for that body. Pilates Physics
> teaches you to see it, so you can adapt what you're teaching and know what
> comes next.

**D. The sharp version, when the piece needs an edge**

> When the load is wrong, no cue can fix it.

**Keep it scoped exactly that way.** Inside its condition the claim is true and
unarguable, and it does not tell a teacher that her cueing is the problem.
Unscoped, as "words do not matter," it is both insulting and false: sometimes a
different cue really is the one that lands, and being good at that is worth
something. Nurture v2 email 3 in
[current-copy/nurture-v2.md](current-copy/nurture-v2.md) is written on the scoped
version. Match it.

**Keep "load" as the one technical word.** Define it once, then use it
everywhere. Every response in this file lands on it, which is what makes them
read as one brand instead of twelve clever answers.

**Open item.** "Progress" is an addition to the current controlling idea in
[peace-framework.md](peace-framework.md), which promises adapting but not a
forward direction. Progression is what answers the seasoned teacher and the
"what do I do in week six" question. The PEACE beats need a review pass with
progression in mind, and [The two roles of load](#the-two-roles-of-load) below is
the material that pass should be built on.

## The two roles of load

Load does not do the same job in every exercise. **The teaching intent decides
which job is running.** Decide the intent first and the load question mostly
answers itself.

| If the intent is | Load's job | What "right" means |
|---|---|---|
| A skill: articulation, connection, coordination, awareness | Facilitate. Load is there to make the skill reachable | The setting that lets them find the movement, which is often more support, not less |
| Strength: loading tissue, building capacity over time | Supply the stimulus. Load has to climb | A setting genuinely harder than last time, in a step they can actually take |

This is what the three beats in **B** are already describing. **Adapt** is load
serving a skill. **Progress** is load as the stimulus. One dial, two different
reasons to touch it.

**Name the intent whenever you write about load.** A load claim with no intent
attached is where this brand contradicts itself. "Correct load, not maximum load"
and "you have to add load over time" are both true, and they read as opposed
until you say which intent you are talking about.

**Strength is a thing, never the thing.** When strength comes up it travels with
what Pilates is already good at: articulation, connection, coordination,
awareness. Pilates Physics is not a strength brand that happens to use reformers.
Progressive overload is one reason load moves. Making a skill reachable is the
other, and it is the one the survey data actually voiced. This supersedes the
"its own lane" framing in
[funnel-strategy-nikki-session.md](funnel-strategy-nikki-session.md), which parked
strength training entirely.

## The enemy underneath

The belief every pushback below is standing on, in Kaleen's words:

> Load is just there. It is background. It is unchangeable.

And that is just not true. Naming it is the sharpest version of the premise. Load
is a teaching tool, exactly like imagery, verbal cues, and hands-on work. It is
the only one of the four that gets treated as fixed.

**The full source line.** "Load is just so often not the lever. It's not a lever
that's considered. It's just there. It's background. It's unchangeable. And
that's just not true." Keep the phrasing close to this. "It's just there" is the
part that lands, and it is hers, not a writer's metaphor.

**Reserved, not spare.** Nurture v2's enemy slot is spent on "if you don't feel
it, you're doing it wrong," and a sequence gets one enemy. Hold this one for the
tripwire sequence or a standalone piece. Do not stack them.

## Possible pushback to physics and Pilates

In her voice, the way she would say it to a friend.

1. "Classical is simple. It's set. I don't need to know these things."
2. "I'm not good at math. That was never my thing."
3. "It takes too long to give options in the moment."
4. "I don't always want to load my clients to the max." *(hidden assumption:
   physics means heavier. It means matched to the intent.)*
5. "If it looks good to me, then they're getting a good workout." *(weakest, and
   the best hook. You can't see load, you have to ask.)*
6. "This is theory. It's not applicable to teaching group classes." *(real, and
   where both low scores came from. Answer it before it gets asked.)*
7. "I've been doing this 15 years. I already adjust for every body by feel."
   *(ICP 2. Not wrong, just not teachable to her team.)*
8. "My training covered springs. I trust my certification." *(weak)*
9. "My studio has the settings posted on the board. I teach the protocol."
10. "My clients don't care why. They came to move and sweat, not to hear a
    lecture."
11. "Every body is different, so there's no formula anyway. What's physics going
    to tell me?"
12. "Isn't this just the spring chart? I can look up a chart."

## Ways to show you know what she's going through

The empathy beat: say the thing she hasn't said out loud.

1. "Your teacher training did exactly what it was supposed to do. Now you learn
   how to adapt the settings you were taught for more people."
2. "The best teachers are the ones who can adapt Pilates for anyone."
3. "Something doesn't feel right, but you don't know how to address it."
4. "Wouldn't this be easier if springs were a set number instead of a range?"
5. "You've watched someone struggle on the setting the manual told you to use. So
   you guessed at a change in cues or springs and hoped."
6. "You know the honest answer is 'it depends.' You're just tired of that being
   the only answer you have."
7. "You can feel the difference on your own body. You just can't put it into
   words yet."
8. "The tall client and the short client are on the same spring, and you already
   know only one of them is right."
9. "You've asked 'how does that feel?' and gotten 'fine,' and known that wasn't
   the whole answer."
10. "You didn't sign up to teach physics. You signed up to teach movement."
11. "You didn't sign up to learn physics. You signed up to learn movement."
12. "Your own body doesn't fit the standard either, and you've been working
    around it for years without saying so."

**On 10 and 11.** These are near-twins on purpose. #10 is for ICP 1 and ICP 2,
who teach. #11 is for ICP 3, who is a student or in training. Never use both in
the same piece.

## The response pattern

Every response below runs the same three beats. Once you can see it, you can
write these live, in the moment.

1. **Concede the true part.** Never argue with her premise. She is usually right
   about something.
2. **Narrow it.** Say what it actually is, or what it isn't. This is where the
   misconception dies.
3. **Land the premise.** Understand load, adapt, progress.

---

## Responses, one per pushback

### 1. "Classical is simple. It's set. I don't need to know these things."

*Pairs with empathy #1 and #4.*

> Classical is set, and that's the strength of it. The order is proven and the
> settings are proven. But the setting is fixed and the body isn't. A 5'1" client
> and a 6'2" client on the same two springs in footwork are doing two different
> amounts of work. Understanding load doesn't change the repertoire. It tells you
> what your client is actually getting out of it.

### 2. "I'm not good at math. That was never my thing."

*Pairs with empathy #10 or #11.*

> Math not your thing? That's okay, and you don't have to do math while you
> teach. Physics is just the why underneath what you're already doing. Pilates
> Physics helps you understand load, so you can adapt exercises to the humans in
> front of you. No calculator, no equations in class.

### 3. "It takes too long to give options in the moment."

*Pairs with empathy #6 and #3.*

> It does take too long when you're generating the options on the spot. That's
> the slow part. When you understand how load changes with the springs, the
> footbar, and body position, the options are already narrowed before you open
> your mouth. You're choosing one, not inventing one. That's a sentence, not a
> pause in the class.

### 4. "I don't always want to load my clients to the max."

*Pairs with empathy #8.*

> You shouldn't, and maximum was never the point. What decides it is what you are
> teaching. When the intent is a skill, load is there to make that skill
> reachable, and plenty of the time that means lighter, or more support, not
> heavier. When the intent is strength, load does have to climb over time, in
> steps small enough that they can actually take them. Understanding load is what
> tells you which of those two you are doing, and which direction to move.

**Note.** This response used to read "more load was never the goal, correct load
is." That is only true under a skill intent, so it was rewritten. See
[The two roles of load](#the-two-roles-of-load).

### 5. "If it looks good to me, then they're getting a good workout."

*Pairs with empathy #9 and #7. This is the strongest hook, so give it the most
air.*

> Form is only one kind of feedback. It doesn't tell you how hard it is. Two
> clients can look identical in footwork and be at completely different efforts,
> and there's no way to see the difference from where you're standing. You have
> to ask. Understanding load is what tells you which question to ask and what to
> do with the answer.

### 6. "This is theory. It's not applicable to teaching group classes."

*Pairs with empathy #8. Answer this before it gets asked.*

> Fair, and if it stayed theory it would be useless to you. Group class is where
> this actually pays off. One setting, ten bodies, thirty seconds. Understanding
> load is how you offer two or three options that are genuinely right for that
> room instead of the same one for everybody. This is the physics, applied.

### 7. "I've been doing this 15 years. I already adjust for every body by feel."

*Pairs with empathy #7. ICP 2.*

> You do, and your instincts are good. That isn't in question. The trouble is
> that "because I said so" doesn't train your junior instructors. Pilates Physics
> puts words to what you already know, so you can hand it to your team instead of
> hoping they pick it up over ten years the way you did.

### 8. "My training covered springs. I trust my certification."

*Pairs with empathy #1.*

> You should trust it. It gave you a starting point, and that was exactly the
> right place to start. But a starting spring is a starting point, not a
> decision. It tells you where to begin. It doesn't tell you what to do when the
> body in front of you doesn't match the manual. That next layer is what this is.

### 9. "My studio has the settings posted on the board. I teach the protocol."

*Pairs with empathy #3.*

> Keep the protocol. This isn't permission to go off script. The board gives you
> the spring. You still choose the footbar, the body position, the cue, and what
> you say to the person who's struggling. Understanding load makes those choices
> deliberate inside the protocol you're already teaching.

### 10. "My clients don't care why. They came to move and sweat, not to hear a lecture."

*Pairs with empathy #9.*

> They don't want a lecture, and you shouldn't give one. What they do want is to
> feel like the class was built for them. Understanding load is what lets you say
> "go down to one red, you'll get more out of it" in one sentence, with real
> confidence behind it. They don't hear physics. They hear a teacher who sees
> them.

### 11. "Every body is different, so there's no formula anyway."

*Pairs with empathy #6.*

> There is no formula, and that's the point. Physics isn't a lookup table, it's
> the set of rules for what changes when the body changes. Longer legs stretch
> the spring further, so the same spring gets heavier. Once you know how the
> variables move, you can reason your way through a body you've never seen before
> instead of guessing.

### 12. "Isn't this just the spring chart? I can look up a chart."

*Pairs with empathy #4.*

> A chart tells you what. It doesn't tell you why, and it can't tell you what to
> do when your client doesn't match it. A chart has one row per exercise. Your
> class has ten bodies. Understanding load is what lets you leave the chart on
> purpose and know you're right.

## Where these go

Each response is a standalone Instagram caption, a nurture email body, or a
workshop FAQ entry.

**Lead with these three.** #5 is the strongest hook. #6 is the objection that
produced both low NPS scores, so it needs answering before it gets asked. #2 is
the barrier the survey data names as the biggest one.

**Pattern for a post or email.** Open on the empathy line, name the pushback in
her words, then run the three beats. Empathy first means the objection dissolves
without you arguing with her.
