# Soundbite Principles (Donald Miller)

Distilled from a conversation between Donald Miller (StoryBrand) and Amy
Porterfield, "Why Great Work Doesn't Sell Itself." These are the general
principles behind the PEACE framework. For how we apply them to Pilates Physics,
see [peace-framework.md](peace-framework.md).

Source: YouTube, https://www.youtube.com/watch/8332jmhhqs8

---

## What a soundbite is

A short, repeatable statement that makes the right person stop and think "that is
exactly what I need," and want to know more. It is not fluff or a clever tagline.
In a messaging campaign it is the piece most businesses are missing: they can
explain what they do for an hour, but they cannot get anyone to sit and listen
for that hour. The soundbite earns the attention first.

## The one principle underneath everything: survival

The only thing that reliably gets attention is survival. Miller's claim is that
everything anyone buys, they buy to increase their chance of survival. So a
soundbite has to convince the reader that you are a "survival asset." The fastest
way to trigger that instinct is to name a problem (a threat) and show that you
have something that mitigates or conquers it.

For a first-world audience, "survival" includes status, confidence, credibility,
and livelihood, not just food and shelter. The job is to connect what you sell to
one of those.

## The five beats: PEACE

Problem, Empathy, Answer, Change, End Result. Notes from the conversation:

- **Problem is by far the most important.** Miller puts it at roughly 50% of the
  whole messaging campaign. Nail this before anything else. It often takes a while
  to find.
- **Change is the optional one.** Miller calls it "icing on the cake" and has
  considered dropping it. He keeps it partly because it makes the acronym spell
  PEACE. Use it when it adds an aspirational identity, skip it when it clutters.
- Using even one beat lifts engagement. Using all five is the goal.

### The "hole" metaphor

Every story is a hero who fell into a hole and needs help getting out.

- **Problem:** names the hole the customer is in, and positions you as the
  solution. Solutions to problems are worth more, so this also raises your value.
- **Empathy:** you stand at the edge and shout "I've been there, I've helped
  many people out of this hole." Empathy does three things at once: it builds a
  bond, it makes you the authority/guide, and it marks you as a survival asset.
- **Answer:** the rope you throw in. Critically, this is literally what you sell:
  "I have a live workshop," "an on-demand course," "a book," "a subscription."
  Name the product that gets them out.
- **Change:** the aspirational identity they take on once they climb out ("a
  confident business owner," "the master of your house," "the coalition of the
  fearless").
- **End Result:** the happy ever after. Paint the new world once the problem is
  solved.

Discipline matters more than cleverness. Once you have the five, memorize them and
work at least one (preferably all five) into every email, post, page, and talk.
"Say it until the right people start saying it back to you."

## Clarity beats completeness: cognitive load

The brain is drawn to simplicity, not nuance. "High cognitive load" is anything
that makes the reader work to figure out what you do. You are always choosing
between low cognitive load but incomplete, and high cognitive load but complete.
Choose low and incomplete to earn curiosity. The nuance can come later, once they
are already listening.

Examples from the talk: "build a wall," "dissolves hair" (on a drain cleaner),
"sleep lab on your finger" (Oura, which does about 12 things but marketed only
sleep and sales jumped), "kids love aquariums," "perfect for a girl's birthday
party." Two rules of thumb Miller repeats: "clarity always wins" and "if you
confuse, you'll lose."

Some soundbites should "think for the customer," putting the conclusion directly
in their head so they do not have to work for it.

## The controlling idea: one thing

A business, like a movie, can only be about one thing. A second thing loses the
audience. If you genuinely have a second thing, start a second business.

It is contextual, though: one controlling idea can have a few sub-angles. Miller's
oil-and-gas example, "protect refinery throughput," had three enemies (downtime,
constraints, complacency) that mapped to three products. One thing, expressed
three ways. Multiple revenue streams are fine as long as they ladder under the one
idea. Anything that does not fit should be spun off.

## Three phases of a campaign

Curiosity, then Enlightenment, then Commitment.

- **Curiosity:** the only job of a website header. A zero cognitive load message
  that convinces the reader you are a survival asset.
- **Enlightenment:** their due diligence. They watch your videos, read your book,
  spend time with you.
- **Commitment:** they pay.

Most brands do enlightenment well but fail at curiosity and commitment, which is
why sales lag. You cannot get someone to the enlightenment phase without piquing
curiosity first.

A finer-grained version of the same ladder is attention, trust, belief, action.
It is a useful lens for a single piece of content rather than a whole campaign.
For how to sustain curiosity past the first line, using open loops, see
[story-framework.md](story-framework.md).

## The three silent questions

A visitor silently asks these in about five seconds, and bounces if they are not
answered:

1. **What do you offer?** Specifically, what can I actually buy (a course, a
   workshop, a membership, hours), not just what you broadly do.
2. **How will it make my life better?**
3. **What do I need to do to buy it?** The concrete next step (buy now, add to
   cart, join the waitlist).

Scarcity is fine. For cart-open / cart-close or capped offers, the "answer" while
closed is simply "join the waitlist."

## One-line summary

You have an offer that works and results that prove it. The right words make the
right people finally see it. Find the controlling idea, state it as one problem in
one sentence, put it at the top of the homepage and the first line of the next
email, and repeat it everywhere.
