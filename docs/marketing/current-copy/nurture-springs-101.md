# Springs 101 Nurture Sequence

Evergreen, six emails, sent to anyone who claims the Springs 101 lead magnet.
Written for **ICP 1, The Curious Practitioner**, with light nods to ICP 2 and 3.

**Cadence:** day 1, 3, 5, 8, 11, 14 (measured from the Kit tag, not from signup,
so the delivery email lands first).

**Destination:** every CTA points to `/education`. That page lists what is
scheduled and falls back to "Join the waitlist" on its own, so the sequence never
needs editing between cohorts. Apparatus-specific links (`/pilates-physics-101`
for Reformer, `/pilates-physics-102` for Chair and Cadillac) are named in the
body copy only where the reader benefits from knowing which is which.

**The arc.** They already accepted the small idea from the primer: a spring is a
slope, not a number. The sequence walks that idea from the equipment to the body.

| # | Beat | The move |
|---|------|----------|
| 1 | Problem, seeded | Prove it to yourself in the calculator |
| 2 | Problem, full weight | The same setting is a different workout |
| 3 | Empathy, then Stakes | Your training was right. Stopping there has a cost |
| 4 | Change | Every setting you touch changes the stretch |
| 5 | Guide, then Plan | Who is teaching this, and the path |
| 6 | Answer, then End Result | The ask, with honest expectations |

## Email 1 · Day 1

**Job:** an unopened lead magnet is a dead lead. Turn the download into an
experience, and let that experience be the Problem beat.

**Subject line options**
1. Do this before you read the primer
2. A red spring is not a single weight
3. Sixty seconds in the calculator

**Preview text:** One minute, two numbers, and the idea the whole primer rests on.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Springs 101 and the Spring Load Calculator are sitting in your portal.

Before you read a word of the primer, do this. It takes about a minute.

Open the calculator. Pick the brand your studio uses. Choose the red spring. Now
drag it out slowly and watch the number climb.

Write down two numbers: what it reads the moment the spring engages, and what it
reads at full stretch.

On a Balanced Body red, those numbers are roughly 8 pounds and 44 pounds.

Same spring. Same rep. More than five times heavier at the end of the carriage's
travel than at the start.

That is the idea the whole primer is built on. A spring is not a weight, it is a
slope. The load climbs the further the spring is stretched.

Which leaves a question your setting sheet cannot answer. If the load depends on
how far the spring gets stretched, and every client stretches it a different
distance, then who in your class is actually on that red?

That one is Wednesday.

Kaleen

## Email 2 · Day 3

**Job:** the Problem beat at full weight. Miller gives this roughly half the
campaign, so this is the email to get right. Bodies first, machines second.

**Subject line options**
1. Two clients on the same red spring
2. Who is actually on that red
3. The setting was the same. The workout was not.

**Preview text:** Back to back clients, one spring, two different loads.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Two clients, back to back. Same reformer, same red spring, same footwork.

Your first client is tall. She presses out to straight legs and the carriage runs
nearly the full length of its track. Your second client is short. She presses out
to the same straight legs, and the carriage travels several inches less.

Now put that against the slope from Monday. The load climbs with stretch. So your
taller client finishes her rep near the top of the climb, somewhere around 40
pounds. Your shorter client finishes lower down the same line, closer to 30.

Neither of them did anything wrong. Neither did you. The setting was the same.
The workout was not.

One student put it better than I can:

> "Short and curvy isn't the norm for my advanced classes, and I feel abandoned
> in them at times because what works for my classmates just doesn't work for me."

She is not describing a bad teacher. She is describing a room where the setting
was the only thing anyone could see.

And it runs deeper than bodies. Put the exact same springs on two different
reformers and they still will not feel the same, because how far a movement
stretches the spring is decided by the machine as much as the spring: how long
the carriage travels, where the spring anchors to the frame, how far the carriage
sits from the spring bar at home, how long the ropes are.

Same spring. Same setting. Different load.

This is the part I most want you to sit with. Your setting sheet tells you which
springs are on. It does not tell you what anyone is lifting.

You cannot read that off the setting. But you can absolutely learn to see it.

Kaleen

## Email 3 · Day 5

**Job:** Empathy first, in full, then the stakes. This is the email that
differentiates the brand and the easiest one to get wrong. Nothing here implies
their training failed them.

**Subject line options**
1. You were taught the settings. That was right.
2. The scaffolding, and what it costs to stay on it
3. Memorizing the script is where you start

**Preview text:** There is a version of this email that blames your teacher
training. This is not it.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

There is a version of this email that tells you your teacher training let you
down. This is not that email.

You were handed the settings and the script for a good reason. Nobody can teach a
full room while inventing from scratch. The standard setting for footwork, the
order of the exercises, the cues you learned by heart. All of it is scaffolding,
and it is the reason you could walk into your first class and actually teach it.

Memorizing the settings and the script is exactly where you start.

It is a starting line, though, not a destination. And here is what staying there
costs, in the ordinary week to week of teaching.

When a body does not fit the standard setting, your only lever is to add a spring
or drop one, and then hope. You know that cannot be the only option, because you
have watched a spring change fix one thing and break another.

When a client asks why this feels so much harder than it did at her old studio,
you have the setting. You do not have the reason. And she can tell.

When you are honest with yourself, some days feel less like teaching and more
like reciting. One instructor described it as regurgitating what she was taught
instead of thinking, and that line has stuck with me because so many people say
a version of it.

And from the outside, you sound like every other teacher running one script for
the whole room. That is the quiet one. It is not a safety problem or a skill
problem, it is a standing-out problem, and it decides who keeps clients.

Not one of those is a failure of effort. They are all the same missing piece.
Somebody gave you the rules and left out the reason underneath them.

That gap is fixable, and it is smaller than it looks from here.

Kaleen

## Email 4 · Day 8

**Job:** the Change beat, taught as mechanism rather than as a list of hacks.
The point is not "try these before the spring," it is "understand that every
setting you touch is changing how far the spring stretches."

**Subject line options**
1. Every setting you touch is changing one thing
2. The one variable you actually control
3. What the footbar is really doing

**Preview text:** The manufacturer sets two of the three numbers. The third one
is yours.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Here is the model from the primer, in one line:

**Force = (k × stretch) + b**

**b** is the starting tension, what the spring already pulls before the carriage
moves. **k** is the rate, how fast the load climbs per inch of travel. **stretch**
is how far the spring actually gets pulled.

Look at what you can and cannot change. The manufacturer set k and b in a factory
years ago. You cannot touch them. Swapping to a different spring swaps you onto a
different line, and that is the only thing a spring change does.

Stretch is yours. And nearly every setting on that machine is a way of changing
it.

There are two ways to do that, and they are worth separating in your head.

**You can move where the movement starts on the curve.** The gear or spring bar
position, the rope length, where the carriage sits at home. These decide how much
tension is already in the spring before anyone moves, which is to say they move
your client up or down the line before the first rep.

**Or you can change how much of the curve the movement covers.** Footbar height,
where you place the body, how far a client is set up from the bar, and the limb
lengths of the person in front of you. These decide how much travel a given
movement produces, which is to say how far up the climb she finishes.

Two clients on the same spring, ending at different points on the same line. That
is the tall and short problem from Wednesday, and it is also the lever. If a
setting is landing wrong for someone, you can move where she starts, or you can
change how far she travels, and both of those change the load without touching a
spring.

There is a third category worth naming so it does not surprise you later. Some
settings, pulley height most of all, change the direction the force pulls rather
than how far the spring stretches. That one needs force vectors to explain
properly, so it is a workshop conversation, not an email.

None of this makes the spring the wrong answer. Sometimes the spring is exactly
the right answer. The difference is that you would be choosing it, instead of
reaching for it because it is the only dial you were handed.

Here is how one instructor put it after the workshop:

> "Changing the footbar or the body position. My go to has always been change the
> springs. Now I will be trying other things first."

Kaleen

## Email 5 · Day 11

**Job:** Guide, then Plan. The only email where Kaleen is on camera. Empathy is
already established across emails 1 through 4, so this one can lean authority.
The origin story disarms the math fear on its own, so do not belabor it.

**Subject line options**
1. I was an engineer before I ever taught a class
2. Why I came at this backwards
3. How this ended up being my problem to solve

**Preview text:** I knew the reformer as a set of forces before I knew it as a
place bodies work.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

I should tell you where all of this came from.

I studied mechanical engineering. In 2013 I took a job as a design engineer at
Balanced Body, and to understand what I was actually designing, I started taking
Pilates. So I came at this backwards from almost every teacher I know. I knew the
reformer as geometry and force before I ever knew it as a place bodies work.

When I started teaching, the thing that kept catching me was how often
instructors are handed a rule with the reason stripped out. Not because anyone
was hiding it. Because certification has an enormous amount of ground to cover in
very little time, and the physics is the first thing cut.

I had the tools to go find the reason. Most teachers were never offered them.
That gap is the entire reason Pilates Physics exists.

Here is what teachers say once it closes.

> "You took an intimidating topic (Physics!) and made it so understandable in a
> Pilates application."

> "It provided me with more understanding and language to help myself and future
> students understand what is happening between their body and the reformer."

> "I feel empowered to make more equipment modifications to better fit MY body's
> needs rather than just following along with a room full of completely different
> bodies than mine."

If math has never been your thing, hold onto that first one. It is the most
common thing people tell me, and it is the whole design brief for how I teach
this.

The path from here is short.

**One.** Springs 101, which you already have. That is the equipment.

**Two.** The workshop for the apparatus you teach on. Pilates Physics 101 for
reformer, 102 for chair and cadillac. That is where the equipment meets the body.

**Three.** Teach from the why, and give your clients the language too.

Kaleen

## Email 6 · Day 14

**Job:** the ask. Answer made concrete, End Result to close, and honest
expectation setting built in. That last part costs a few of the wrong buyers and
protects the experience for everyone else.

**Subject line options**
1. What the workshop is, and what it is not
2. The rest of it
3. Every body in your room, on purpose

**Preview text:** Straight answer on what you get and what you do not.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Two weeks, three ideas.

A spring is a slope, not a number. The same setting is a different workout on
every body. And nearly every setting you touch is really changing how far that
spring gets stretched.

That is the first layer. The workshop is the rest of it.

**What it is.** One live session, plus the recording, so you can go back to it as
often as you want. Pilates Physics 101 covers the reformer. Pilates Physics 102
covers the chair and the cadillac. You get the physics behind the settings and,
just as importantly, how to teach with it: the cues, the options you can offer a
mixed room, and the language to hand a client so she can choose her own load
instead of comparing herself to the person next to her.

No math background required. That is not a marketing line, it is a design
constraint I hold myself to.

**What it is not.** It is not a movement class. You will not be on the equipment
during the session, and if a practical class is what you are after, this is not
it. It is also not a set of hacks. It is the reasoning underneath, which is the
part that keeps working on bodies you have not met yet.

If that is what you want, here is the End Result. Every student in your room gets
a class built for her body, not for the model in your training manual. She feels
the difference because the class was built around her. And she trusts you,
because you can tell her what her body is feeling and why.
