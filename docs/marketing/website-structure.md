# StoryBrand Website & Landing Page Structure

This is the structural companion to [peace-framework.md](peace-framework.md). The
PEACE file tells you *what to say*. This file tells you *where it goes* on a page
and *in what order*. Use it when auditing an existing page or laying out a new
one.

The structure comes from Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework: the same SB7 story
beats that drive our PEACE soundbites, arranged into the sections of a page. Our
PEACE beats map cleanly onto it, so this file notes which beat feeds each section.

**Sources this is built from:** the section-by-section wireframe from Agency Boon,
the SB7 explainer from Chatterbuzz (including the Harry Potter walkthrough below),
and the web-design tips from Smashed Avo. Full links are at the bottom.

---

## The SB7 framework in one glance (the Harry Potter version)

Before the page layout, the story logic. Every StoryBrand page is built on seven
beats: a **character** who has a **problem** meets a **guide** who gives them a
**plan** and **calls them to action** that helps them avoid **failure** and ends
in **success**. The customer is the hero. Your brand is the guide, never the hero.

Harry Potter is the cleanest illustration of the seven beats:

| SB7 beat | In Harry Potter | On our page |
|----------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Character (Hero)** | Harry, the boy who wants to belong and survive | The instructor who wants to teach any body with confidence |
| **Problem** | Death Eaters attack; good versus evil. External threat, internal fear for his friends, philosophical stakes | Taught what springs to set but never why. External (a body does not fit the setting), internal (feeling exposed), philosophical (students deserve a teacher who knows why) |
| **Guide** | Hagrid and Dumbledore. Trusted mentors who have been there before | Pilates Physics. Empathy plus authority, the Yoda not the Luke |
| **Plan** | The steps and lessons Harry follows to be ready | Our simple 3-step path to registering and learning |
| **Call to Action** | Harry realizes he is the one who must act | Register now / join the waitlist |
| **Failure (Stakes)** | Voldemort takes over the Wizarding World | Keep defaulting to spring swaps, keep guessing, blend in with every rote teacher |
| **Success** | If Harry succeeds, there is harmony | Teach with intention, adapt to any body, explain the why |

The reason this matters: human brains are wired to follow story structure. When a
page does not follow it, people disengage. The layout below is just these seven
beats poured into page sections in the order a visitor can absorb them.

Note the one trap: **the customer is the hero, not the brand.** Dumbledore never
tries to be Harry. If our copy makes Pilates Physics the hero, it breaks. See the
mistakes list below.

## The page, section by section

This is the canonical StoryBrand homepage or landing page order. Sections 7 to 9
are optional depending on the page. Each visitor skims, so every section needs a
clear header that carries meaning on its own.

### 1. Header / Hero

**The most important section. Most visitors never scroll past it.** It has to pass
the "grunt test," which is Miller's version of our
[three silent questions](soundbite-principles.md): a caveman should be able to
grunt back what you do, how it helps, and how to get it, within about five
seconds.

Answer three questions above the fold:

1. What do you offer?
2. How will it make the visitor's life better?
3. What do they do next?

**Craft notes:**
- Keep text minimal. Clarity beats cleverness. "People buy the products they
  understand the fastest, not the best."
- Place a direct call to action twice: once in the upper-right nav, once in the
  center near the headline.
- Use a background image that shows the aspirational identity the customer wants,
  or the product in action, not a generic stock shot.
- **Our fills:** lead the headline with the **Problem** beat. Use the **Answer**
  beat for the sub-headline. See [peace-framework.md](peace-framework.md).

### 2. Stakes

**What goes wrong if they do nothing.** This is loss aversion. Show the cost of
not working with you.

**Craft notes:**
- Keep it short. Name the external, internal, and philosophical problem.
- Quantify the cost in dollars or concrete loss where you can. Financial or
  concrete stakes lift conversion.
- Include a call to action.
- **Our fill:** this is the **Problem** beat escalated. The stakes are
  professional, not literal (see the survival-asset section of the PEACE file):
  blending in, guessing, losing credibility in front of experienced clients.

### 3. Value Proposition

**Your offering as the solution to the problem you just raised.** Usually a
"value stack": three or four short, icon-topped statements, each a distinct
benefit.

**Craft notes:**
- Format each as a short header plus a one-line benefit.
- Tie each straight back to a pain point from the Stakes section.
- **Our fill:** pull from the **Change** and **End Result** beats. What shifts and
  what they walk away with.

### 4. Guide

**Show empathy, then authority.** This is where you prove you are Dumbledore. Two
jobs, in this order:

- **Empathy:** mirror the customer's own language, name the struggle, use "we
  understand" before you list the problem. Validate before you sell.
- **Authority:** testimonials (three short ones is the sweet spot), client or
  brand logos, quantified results, awards or badges (put badges low on the page).

**Craft notes:**
- Balance the two. Over-indexing on credentials kills relatability.
- Use the customer's real words. Our swipe file is
  [customer-language.md](customer-language.md).
- **Our fill:** the **Empathy** beat, then the authority proof. "Memorizing the
  settings is exactly where you start. The nuance is where you grow."

### 5. Plan

**The simple path that removes fear of complexity.** Three steps is the default
(up to six if needed). Each step is short, active, and says what the customer
gets from it.

**Craft notes:**
- Lay it out visually, as three numbered columns.
- A "process plan" (the steps to buy or start) works for everyone. An "agreement
  plan" (the promises you make to reduce risk) is an optional add-on.
- **Our fill:** the steps mirror the funnel below. Typically: 1) grab the free
  Springs 101 guide, 2) register for the workshop (live plus the recording),
  3) teach with intention. On a page whose CTA is the lead magnet, the plan can
  stop at "start with Springs 101" and let the nurture sequence carry the rest.

### 6. Explanatory Paragraph

**The long-form block for the skimmer who needs more before committing, and for
SEO.** A text section under a clear header that synthesizes the BrandScript.

**Craft notes:**
- Show the first paragraph in full, make the rest expandable so it does not
  crowd the page.
- Weave in descriptive, searchable language (location, apparatus, service terms)
  without going full keyword-salad. StoryBrand clarity and SEO can coexist:
  "the physics behind the springs, for Reformer and Chair instructors" reads
  human and still carries keywords.

### 7. Lead Generator / Transitional CTA (optional but recommended)

**A low-commitment offer for people not ready to buy.** A free, valuable resource
(guide, checklist, assessment) traded for an email. Builds reciprocity and
captures the not-yet-ready.

**Craft notes:**
- Pair every direct CTA ("Register now") with a transitional one ("Get the free
  guide") somewhere on the page.
- **Our fill:** Springs 101 is our lead magnet and transitional CTA. It captures
  the email and feeds the nurture sequence. See the funnel note below for when it
  leads the page versus supports it.

### 8. Video (optional)

**A 2 to 3 minute story piece.** Scripted, not improvised, customer-centric, based
on the BrandScript. Keep it under three minutes with a compelling title.

### 9. Price / Product Choices (optional)

**Offerings in a digestible, comparable format.** Show your best three packages or
the most-purchased options.

**Craft notes:**
- People tend to pick the middle option, so position the tier you most want sold
  in the middle.
- Give each choice its own direct CTA and a bite-sized description.

### 10. Junk Drawer (Footer)

**Everything secondary, kept out of the main path.** About, blog, careers, FAQ,
contact, social links.

**Why it exists:** it lets you keep the header nav short (five links max) so the
primary path stays clean, while still giving thorough visitors somewhere to go.
Reduces decision paralysis up top.

## Craft tips and rules of thumb

Pulled mostly from the Smashed Avo web-design guide, filtered to what fits our
brand.

**Message and copy:**
- Most marketing fails because it is too complicated. Write so plainly a caveman
  gets it. "Even a caveman" is the bar.
- Focus on one main problem, not a list. Address its external, internal, and
  philosophical layers.
- Use the customer's own words, lifted from reviews and surveys. See
  [customer-language.md](customer-language.md).
- Every section needs a header that means something on its own, because visitors
  skim, not read.

**Calls to action:**
- Two types: **direct** (Register, Buy, Schedule) and **transitional** (Download
  the free guide, Watch the demo). Use both on the page.
- Place a direct CTA above the fold, after the plan, in the footer, and in the
  nav. Repetition is the point.
- **Our two CTAs are fixed, but which one leads is not.** See the funnel note
  below before you pick the primary CTA for a page.

### Our funnel: which CTA leads

We run one funnel with two calls to action, and the page's job decides which one
is primary:

- **Transitional CTA (the lead magnet): Springs 101.** A free guide traded for an
  email. This is the low-commitment entry point that feeds the nurture sequence.
- **Direct CTA: workshop registration.** The high-commitment ask (Pilates Physics
  101 for Reformer, 102 for Chair and Cadillac).

Which one is the hero button depends on the page:

- **Lead-magnet-led pages** (cold traffic, top-of-funnel, blog posts, social
  landing pages): make Springs 101 the primary CTA. The visitor is not ready to
  buy. Capture the email, let the nurture sequence sell the workshop. Workshop
  registration can still appear lower as the secondary option.
- **Registration-led pages** (a live cohort is open, warm traffic, the workshop
  sales page itself): make registration the primary CTA. Keep Springs 101 present
  as the transitional catch for anyone not ready, so no one leaves empty-handed.

Rule of thumb: **every page offers both, and neither should ever be missing.** The
only decision is which one gets the visual weight.

**Design:**
- Limit main navigation to five links. Everything else goes in the footer.
- Generous white space, clear visual hierarchy, no clutter.
- Show successful outcomes in imagery. Real customer photos beat stock. Before and
  after works.
- Mobile-responsive and fast-loading are non-negotiable.

**Testimonials and proof:**
- Tell transformation stories, not just "they were happy." Problem, what changed,
  measurable result.
- Names and photos when you can get them.

## The five mistakes to audit against

When reviewing a page, these are the failure modes to catch first:

1. **The brand is the hero.** If the copy centers the company's greatness instead
   of the customer's transformation, fix it. We are the guide.
2. **The message is overcomplicated.** Jargon and cleverness lose people. Cut to
   clarity.
3. **Weak or missing CTAs.** If the next step is unclear or buried, conversions
   leak. Make it obvious and repeat it.
4. **The framework is followed too literally.** It is a guide, not a
   fill-in-the-blank. Adapt the order and sections to the page's job.
5. **Authority and empathy are out of balance.** All credentials and no empathy
   reads cold. All empathy and no proof reads soft. Lead with empathy, back it
   with authority.

## Quick audit checklist

Run any page against this:

- [ ] **Hero passes the grunt test** in five seconds: what, how it helps, what to
      do next.
- [ ] The primary CTA is visible above the fold and repeated down the page.
- [ ] **Both CTAs are present:** Springs 101 (transitional) and workshop
      registration (direct). The right one leads for this page's traffic.
- [ ] **Stakes** are named. The visitor sees what inaction costs.
- [ ] **Value** is stated as three or four clear benefits, tied to the stakes.
- [ ] **Guide** section shows empathy first, then authority (proof, logos,
      results).
- [ ] **Plan** is three simple steps, laid out visually.
- [ ] Nav has five links or fewer; the rest is in the footer.
- [ ] The **customer is the hero** throughout, not the brand.
- [ ] Every section header carries meaning on its own for skimmers.
