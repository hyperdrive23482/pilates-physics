Three drafts to choose from. This is email 1 of the five-email sequence outlined
with Nikki in [funnel-strategy-nikki-session.md](../funnel-strategy-nikki-session.md):
POV → enemy → misdiagnosis → real diagnosis → origin story.

**The job of this email.** State your point of view. Here is how springs are
normally taught, here is why that is incomplete, here is what I believe instead.
Nikki's note: the goal is to make them think differently and to connect with your
words, and it is allowed to be a little edgy.

**What it must not do.**

- Do not solve anything. The misdiagnosis is email 3, the real diagnosis is email
  4. This email plants a belief and leaves it sitting there.
- Do not name the enemy in full. That is email 2. Each draft ends on an open loop
  pointing at it.
- One idea only: **a spring is not one weight.**

**Voice note.** Nikki said "edgy," and [voice-and-messaging.md](../voice-and-messaging.md)
says never imply a teacher's training failed them. Both drafts below resolve that
the same way: the edge points at the convention, never at the reader. The chart
they were handed was right. It was just the first layer, and nobody mentioned
there was a second one.

## Version A: "I bet you learned it this way too"

The straight-down-the-middle POV email, closest to Nikki's outline. Names the
convention, states the belief, backs it with one number. Safest of the three and
the easiest to edit.

**Subject line options**
1. I bet you learned it this way too
2. What teacher training told you about springs
3. The spring chart is not wrong. It is incomplete.

**Preview text:** The rule you were handed is true, right up until it isn't.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Somewhere in your training, someone handed you a chart.

Footwork is two reds and a blue. Feet in straps is one red. Red is heavy, blue is
light, and if a client is struggling you take one off.

I got that chart too. I taught off it for years, and I still use it as a starting
point, because it is a genuinely useful piece of information.

It is just not the whole piece.

Here is the part nobody mentioned: a spring is not one weight.

Go check it yourself, it takes a minute. Open the calculator, pick the brand your
studio uses, choose a red, and drag it out slowly. On a Balanced Body red, it
reads about 8 pounds the moment it engages and about 44 pounds at full stretch.

Same spring. Same rep. More than five times heavier at the end than at the start.

Which means your chart is describing the machine. It is not describing your
client. The number you hang on the reformer and the load your client actually
feels are two different numbers, and the gap between them is decided by how far
that particular body stretches that particular spring.

I think that is the single most useful thing a Pilates teacher can know, and
almost nobody is taught it.

There is a phrase that gets said in studios all the time that comes directly out
of not knowing this. It is well meant and it does real damage. That one is next
time.

Kaleen

## Version B: "The duet"

Scene first, thesis second. Opens on the five foot two and six foot two duet
Nikki suggested, so the reader recognizes herself before she gets the idea.
Warmest of the three, and the most forwardable.

**Subject line options**
1. The five foot two wife and the six foot two husband
2. One spring, two people, two different workouts
3. You have taught this duet

**Preview text:** Same setting, same footwork, and only one of them is working.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

You have taught this duet.

She is five foot one. He is six foot tall. They booked together, they are on matching reformers, and you set them both to your standard footwork springs.

Two sets in, he is feeling the burn and she is casually chatting with you about their
weekend.  

She jokes that he really needs more Pilates to get stronger.

For a long time I assumed that he was really just not grasping the work.  Or maybe my cues weren't landing for him the way they did for her.

That's not it at all.

A spring is not one weight. It gets heavier the further it is stretched. And they were each extending the springs very different amounts!

He presses out to straight legs, stretching the springs about 21 inches. She presses out to straight legs, creating the same shape, but the carriage travels only 13 and a half inches.

On my baseline Balanced Body footwork springs (red, red, green), they experience a difference in resistance of more than 20 pounds at full extension.

Even gearing him out one slot (or lowering his footbar) keeps the maximum load gap at 14 pounds.

They were on the same springs, but never had the same experience, no matter my training manual said.

There is a thing we say to clients in exactly this moment, when the setting is
right and the person still cannot feel it. I want to talk about that one next
time, because I think it is the most quietly harmful sentence in this
industry.

Kaleen

## Version C: "One sentence"

Short, flat, declarative. Almost no scene setting. This is the edgiest of the
three and the most confident, and it lives or dies on the reader trusting a
stranger's flat assertion. Best if you want the sequence to open with a jolt.

**Subject line options**
1. Springs are not one weight
2. The number on your spring chart is not the load
3. Read this before you teach today

**Preview text:** One sentence, and everything I teach sits on top of it.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

I am going to give you one sentence, and then I am going to spend the next two
weeks showing you what it does to your teaching.

Springs are not one weight.

A red is not "a red." It is a slope. It gets heavier the further it is stretched,
and it does that whether or not anyone told you. On a Balanced Body red: about 8
pounds at the moment it engages, about 44 at full stretch.

So when you put a room of ten people on two reds and a blue, you have not given
ten people the same exercise. You have given ten people ten different loads, and
the tall ones are working hardest without either of you knowing it.

Every setting sheet in this industry is written as though that is not true.

I want to be precise about this, because I am not telling you your training was
wrong. The settings you learned are a good starting place and I still teach from
them. But they describe what to hang on the machine. They say nothing about what
the person on the machine is going to feel, and the person is the entire job.

That is what I believe, and it is the thing everything else I teach rests on:
your client's experience of a spring has almost nothing to do with the color of
it.

Once you have seen that, some very common Pilates advice starts to sound
different. Next time I want to talk about one line in particular. You have
said it. I have said it. It is the single line I would most like to retire.

Kaleen

## Notes for whichever you pick

- **Pick the number, then check it.** All three use the Balanced Body red at
  roughly 8 lb engaged and 44 lb at full stretch. Confirm those against the
  calculator data before sending, and swap to whichever brand is most common in
  your list if it is not Balanced Body.
- **The `/portal` link** is a placeholder. Point it wherever the calculator
  actually lands for a new subscriber.
- **No day references.** Emails point at each other with "next time" and "last
  email," never "Wednesday," so the drip schedule can change without touching
  copy. See the rule in [voice-and-messaging.md](../voice-and-messaging.md).
- **All three end on the same open loop** into email 2, the enemy: "if you don't
  feel it, you're doing it wrong." Keep the loop wording in email 1 vague. The
  payoff is that email 2 names it.
