**The job of this email.** Name the wrong fix and stop. Nikki's framing: "the
misdiagnosis is, you think the problem is X. You're not going to tell them what Y
is yet." Her warning about this exact email was blunt, that you will want to
name the problem and the solution in one go, and that you should not, because
you will rewrite it sixteen times and make yourself crazy.

**The misdiagnosis, agreed on the call:** *you think if you can explain it
better, they'll get it better.* Nikki's response to that was "it's perfect."

**Kaleen's calibration note.** Cues are sometimes genuinely the answer. The point
is not that language never works, it is that load is a lever most teachers do not
know they have, so language gets asked to do a job it cannot always do. Version A
has been revised to hold that line. B and C are still the original, more absolute
drafts.

**What it must do.**

- **Pay off email 2's open loop.** Email 2 promised "the thing almost all of us
  reach for instead" and called it "often the wrong thing." All three drafts name
  it early: better words. More cues, better imagery, more anatomy.
- **Protect cueing as a skill.** Kaleen teaches cues, and the workshop now ships
  a steal-my-cues takeaway. So the argument is never "cueing is useless." It is
  that language is the wrong instrument for this particular problem, because no
  sentence changes what a spring weighs.
- **Keep the reader competent.** She is not bad at explaining. She is very likely
  excellent at it, which is exactly why she keeps reaching for it.

**What it must not do.**

- **Do not deliver the real diagnosis.** That is email 4: what actually sets the
  load for each body, and the fact that it is knowable and adjustable. Email 3
  ends at "you have been solving a load problem with language."
- **Do not re-teach email 1.** She has already seen the duet and the twenty pound
  gap. Reference it, do not re-explain it.

**One structural note worth deciding.** Email 1 already showed load differing
between two bodies, so email 4 cannot present "load differs" as a revelation. The
arc that keeps each email carrying something new is: email 1 proves it happens in
one exercise, email 3 says language cannot fix it, email 4 shows it is happening
in every exercise all class long and that the variables are knowable. All three
drafts below are written to hand off to that version of email 4.

## Version A: "The cue pile"

The reflex caught in the act, in real time, escalating. Closest to Nikki's
outline and the most direct sequel to email 2.

**Softened, per Kaleen's note:** cues are sometimes the answer, but load is often
the lever nobody knows is there. The original absolute version of this draft is
preserved below under "Version A, first pass," so you can dial back toward it.

**Subject line options**
1. The thing we reach for instead
2. You have said it four different ways
3. The lever you were never handed

**Preview text:** When they cannot feel it, we reach for better words. Sometimes that works.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Here is the thing almost all of us reach for.

A client says they cannot feel footwork in their legs. So you say it again, differently.

Press through the whole foot. Then, imagine you are pushing the wall away. Then
you get more anatomical, because maybe they need the detail. Then you get more
poetic, because maybe they need the image. Then you demo it. Then you put a hand
on their leg to cue it by touch.

And sometimes that works. Sometimes a different version of a cue is the one that
lands, they light up, and it really was a communication problem. That happens,
and it is worth being good at.

But sometimes you go through all six of those and they say "oh, maybe, a little,"
which you both know is what people say when they want to move on.

That is the moment I want to talk about, because that is where most of us
conclude we need better words.

I want to be clear that reaching for words is not a failure of skill. That
escalation is a teacher being good at their job. You had a client who was not
getting a result, and you cycled through every tool you had.

It is just that all six of those tools were different versions of the same tool.

And you already know from the duet we talked about earlier what they may be up against. A smaller person
on the same springs as the larger person beside them is working against genuinely different
resistance. Not a little different. Meaningfully different.

Now, a cue can change the sensed load in two ways. If your words change how far they
travel, the load changes with them. Or you could cue them to make a different shape with their body, which creates different loads not directly related to spring tension.

Both are valid adjustments.

What a cue cannot do is close a gap that large.

It cannot change how the spring load compares to your client's own body weight.

Even when maximum load is not the goal, the spring is still doing a job of its own. A cue sends information to your client's ears. The spring sends it straight through their body. Some people need more of that input before the work registers, and some need less.

So you can cue them beautifully, and they can execute it beautifully, and they will be beautifully performing an exercise that is still too light for them to feel it as much as you want them to.

None of this means your cueing is the problem. Cueing is the skill you have, so it is the skill you reach for, including in the moments when the thing standing between them and the sensation is not a sentence. It is a number.

Which raises the obvious question. When words are not the lever, what is, and how
would you know which situation you are in before the class rather than after?

That is next time, and it is the email this whole thing has been building toward.

Kaleen

P.S. This week, if someone is performing the exercise well but doesn't feel it, ask yourself whether changing the load might be the next experiment to try and help them feel something different.

### Version A, first pass

The original, more absolute version of the lines above, kept in case the
softened draft reads as hedging. The difference is concentrated in five places.

| Beat | First pass | Softened |
|------|-----------|----------|
| The cue escalation | Goes straight to "oh, maybe, a little" | Adds "sometimes that works... it really was a communication problem" before the failure case |
| The tools line | "every tool in that list is made of language" | "all six of those tools were the same tool" |
| The setup | "what she is actually up against" | "what she **may** be up against" |
| Can a cue change load | "No sentence you say changes that number." | "A cue can move that number a little... What a cue cannot do is close a twenty pound gap." |
| The verdict | "You do not have a communication problem. You have a load problem, and load does not respond to words." | "None of this means your cueing is the problem... the thing standing between her and the sensation is not a sentence. It is a number." |

The fourth row is the one that changed on accuracy, not just tone. A cue that
changes range of motion genuinely does change load, so "no sentence changes that
number" was overstated. The softened line is both gentler and more correct, and
it quietly previews that range of motion is one of the dials, which is email 4's
territory.

## Version B: "The client who did everything right"

Puts the misdiagnosis in a scene. Warmest of the three, and the one that lets the
reader off the hook most gently, because the client in it is compliant and the
teaching in it is good.

**Subject line options**
1. She did everything you asked
2. The client who followed every cue
3. What if the cue was never the problem

**Preview text:** Perfect compliance, perfect cueing, and she still could not feel it.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

I had a client once who was the easiest person in the world to teach.

She listened. She adjusted the first time I asked. If I said press through the
whole foot, she pressed through the whole foot, and I could see her doing it
correctly from across the room.

And she never felt footwork in her legs. Not once, in months.

So I did what I think you would have done, and what I had been trained to do. I
assumed the message was not landing. I found new words. I collected cues from
every teacher I admired. I got more precise about anatomy, then less precise and
more visual, then I filmed myself teaching to find out what I was doing wrong.

None of it worked, because none of it was ever going to work.

She was doing the exercise correctly. That was the part I could not get past for
a long time. Her form was not the variable, her attention was not the variable,
and my explanation was not the variable, because all three were already fine.

You saw the duet. You know that two people on identical springs can be working
against loads more than twenty pounds apart. She was a small person on the
standard setting, which is to say she was doing an exercise I had accidentally
made too easy for her to feel, and then I spent months trying to talk her into
feeling it.

That is the misdiagnosis, and it is a generous one. When a client does not get a
result, good teachers blame their own explanation first. It is a decent instinct.
It is also why so many of us have a folder of cues and a client who still cannot
feel her legs.

Better words were never going to move that number.

So what does? What actually decides what she feels, and how would you know it
before the class instead of after?

That is next time.

Kaleen

P.S. If you want to see the gap for yourself before then, the calculator is still
sitting in your portal. Put your own footwork springs in and compare a short
client to a tall one.

## Version C: "A category error"

Flat and declarative, thesis first. The most confident of the three, and the most
likely to get screenshotted. Best if you want this email to feel like a
correction rather than a story.

**Subject line options**
1. You do not have a communication problem
2. Words cannot move a spring
3. The wrong tool, used expertly

**Preview text:** You have been solving the wrong kind of problem, very well.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Most Pilates teachers I meet think they have a communication problem.

They tell me some version of the same thing. My clients do not feel it where I
want them to. I must not be explaining it well enough. I need better cues, better
imagery, a better way to describe what I mean.

I believed that for years, and I want to name it plainly, because it is the
single most expensive wrong turn in this work.

Language is not the instrument here.

A cue can change where someone puts their attention. It can change alignment,
tempo, breath, sequence, and effort. Those are real, and they are worth being
good at. I teach cueing, and I will hand you mine.

But a cue cannot change what a spring weighs. That number is set by the equipment
and by how far the body in front of you stretches it, and it is completely
indifferent to how beautifully you describe the movement.

So when a client cannot feel an exercise, and the reason is that she is on
materially less load than the person beside her, better words are not a partial
solution. They are the wrong category of solution. You can execute them
flawlessly and get nothing, which is exactly what happens, and then you conclude
you need to execute them better.

You already saw this in the duet. Same springs, more than twenty pounds apart at
full extension. No sentence in the English language closes that gap.

None of this means you are bad at explaining things. It usually means the
opposite. Explaining is the skill you have, so it is the skill you keep applying,
including to problems that are not made of explanation.

The real question is what is actually setting that load, client by client, and
whether you can know it in advance rather than discovering it in the moment.

You can. That is next time.

Kaleen
