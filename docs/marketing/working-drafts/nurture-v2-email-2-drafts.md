## Version A: "The sentence"

The straight payoff. Names the line, takes it apart, argues the harm. Closest to
Nikki's outline and the most direct sequel to email 1.

**Subject line**

1. "If you don't feel it, you're doing it wrong"

**Preview text:** The line we reach for when the setting is right and they still cannot feel it.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Here is the sentence I promised you.

*"If you don't feel it, you're doing it wrong."*

I have said it. You may have said it. It gets said in studios every day, by
teachers who are good at their jobs and care enormously about the people in front
of them.

And look at what it actually does.

A client tells you she is not feeling footwork where you want her to. That is real
information about her body, offered to you honestly, which is exactly what you
want from a client.

And the sentence takes that information and hands it straight back to her as a
personal failing. Not "something is off in the setup." Something is off in you.

So she stops telling you. She decides she is bad at this, or that her body is the
problem, and maybe she even stops coming. You have lost the the chance to continue sharing the magic of Pilates with her.

Now put that next to the duet we talked about last email. She is five foot one on the same springs as the six
foot man beside her, which means she is genuinely, measurably on a lighter load
than he is. She is not doing it wrong. She is doing it correctly against less
resistance.

The sentence is not cruel. It is just incomplete. It is what you say when memorized
settings are the only information you have, because if the setting is right
and the feeling is missing, the person must be the variable, right?

She is not the only variable to consider.

So what do we reach for instead? Almost all of us reach for the same thing, and I
think it is often the wrong thing. That is next time.

Kaleen

P.S. Hit reply and tell me the line repeated over and over in our industry that makes you wince. I am collecting them, and this one is not the only one.

## Version B: "Her side of it"

Leads with a real student's words, then names the enemy. Warmest of the three and
the hardest to argue with, because the harm is quoted rather than asserted.

**Subject line options**
1. "I feel abandoned in them at times"
2. What she is not telling you
3. The quiet cost of one sentence

**Preview text:** A student told me this, and I have not been able to put it down.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

A student wrote this to me, about the classes she takes:

> "Short and curvy isn't the norm for my advanced classes, and I feel abandoned
> in them at times because what works for my classmates just doesn't work for
> me."

Read that again. Not "the class was too hard." Abandoned.

She is not describing a bad teacher. I would bet the teacher in that room was
warm, prepared, and attentive. She is describing a room where the setting was the
only thing anyone could see, and where the gap between her experience and
everyone else's had no explanation available.

So it got explained the way it usually gets explained. Here is the sentence I
promised you last time:

*"If you don't feel it, you're doing it wrong."*

I have said it. Most of us have. And when you say it to a client who is quietly
on a different load than the person beside her, you are not correcting her form.
You are confirming the thing she already suspected, which is that everyone else
gets this and she does not.

She was on the same springs. She was never on the same workout.

I want to be careful here, because I am not telling you that cueing does not
matter or that form does not matter. They do. This is not a bad idea, it is an
incomplete one. It is the answer you land on when the load is invisible, and the
load is invisible in almost every training manual I have ever read.

There is a better answer. It is more straightforward than you would think, and I
am going to walk you to it.

But first, next time, I want to talk about the thing we try instead. Because I
tried it for years.

Kaleen

P.S. That quote is from a Pilates Physics student, shared with permission. She is
also a teacher.

## Version C: "The setting sheet"

Goes after the belief rather than the phrase. Most argumentative of the three,
and the most quotable on social. Best if you want the sequence to have a real
opponent rather than a bad habit.

**Subject line options**
1. Two reds and a blue, for everyone
2. The setting sheet was never the truth
3. What your manual left out

**Preview text:** It is not a bad rule. It is a rule with a missing column.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Every Pilates teacher I know was handed a version of the same document. Footwork
is two reds and a blue. Feet in straps is one red. Written as fact, with no
mention of who is lying on the carriage.

That document is the thing I am against.

Not because it is wrong. It is a reasonable starting place and I still use one.
It is because of what it implies, which is that a setting is a fixed amount of
work, the same for every person you hand it to.

Follow that belief far enough and you end up at the sentence I promised you last
time:

*"If you don't feel it, you're doing it wrong."*

That sentence is what is left when the setting is treated as the truth. If two
reds and a blue is a known quantity, and she is not feeling it, then the only
remaining variable is her. Her focus. Her abs. Her effort.

But two reds and a blue is not a known quantity. It is a slope, and where each
client lands on that slope depends on how far she pushes the carriage. Same
document, same springs, different load. On my baseline footwork springs, more
than twenty pounds of difference between two adults in the same class.

Your training gave you the column that lists the springs. It did not give you the
column that tells you what those springs weigh for the person actually on them.
That column exists. It is knowable. Most of us were simply never shown it.

Here is what interests me most, though. When the setting is right and the client
still cannot feel it, teachers do not usually blame the client. We blame
ourselves, and we reach for one particular fix.

I did it for years, and it did not work. Next time.

Kaleen
