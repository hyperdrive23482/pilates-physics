# Nurture v2, Email 4: the real diagnosis

Three drafts to choose from. Email 4 of the five-email sequence in
[current-copy/nurture-v2.md](../current-copy/nurture-v2.md).

> **Second pass.** The first pass aimed this email at equipment settings and made
> the payoff "you can predict the load before class." Both were wrong. It pointed
> the reader at an apparatus product when the next thing they are sold is a body
> product, and foresight is not what teachers actually want. This pass fixes
> both. The discarded drafts are at the bottom under "First pass, retired."

**The job of this email.** Nikki's line: "here's what it actually is, make sense
of their experience." Three emails have said "not that." This one says "this."

**The diagnosis: it is the body, not the machine.** The root belief from the call
was "springs are not one weight, therefore all of your people feel different
loads." So the real diagnosis is about the person meeting the spring. Two things
about a body change the number it meets:

1. **How far they move the spring.** Height and limb length. They have already
   seen this in the duet, so this email generalizes it from footwork to every
   exercise, all class long.
2. **How their own body weight sits in the equation.** This is the half they were
   taught wrong, and the half that stays open. It is the tripwire.

**The payoff, corrected.** Not "you can predict this before class." It is:

- You can **explain to a client what they are feeling and why**, in plain
  language, instead of telling them they are doing it wrong.
- You can **adapt with confidence in the moment**, when a client says they are
  not feeling it where you want them to.

That matches what students actually report. From
[customer-language.md](../customer-language.md): "It provided me with more
understanding and language to help myself and future students understand what is
happening between their body and the reformer." And Nikki's value proposition:
when your clients feel it in the right places, they keep coming back.

**What it must not do.**

- **Do not resolve body weight.** Name it, say the common understanding of it is
  backwards, move on. That is the mini course.
- **Do not inventory the equipment settings.** Gear, ropes, footbar, and carriage
  position are Pilates Physics 101's territory. Naming one in passing is fine.
  Cataloguing them gives away the workshop and points at the wrong product.
- **Do not sell.** Email 5 is the origin story. The cart opens after that.
- **Do not teach force vectors.** Pulley height changes the direction of pull and
  needs diagrams. Naming it as a workshop conversation is honest.

## Version A: "What you say instead"

Opens in the exact moment the sequence has been circling, a client saying they
cannot feel it, and hands over both halves of the answer: the sentence to say and
the change to make. The most direct payoff of email 3's closing question.

**Subject line options**
1. What to say when they cannot feel it
2. The answer is not a better cue
3. You will know what to change

**Preview text:** Not a cue. A sentence, and a change you can actually make.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

A client tells you they cannot feel footwork in their legs.

Here is what I would say now, and it takes about ten seconds.

"That makes sense. You're shorter than most people I teach, so you're not
stretching these springs as far, which means you're meeting less resistance than
the person next to you even though we're on the same setting. Let's fix that."

Then you change something. Fewer springs and a longer press, a different setup so
they travel farther, or a heavier spring they can actually reach the end of.

Notice what just happened, because it is the whole point of these emails.

You did not tell them they were doing it wrong. You did not reach for a fifth
cue. You told them what was happening between their body and the machine, and
then you changed it.

That is what the physics is for. Not so you can do arithmetic while you teach.
So you have something true and kind to say in the moment, and so you know what to
reach for next.

Here is the model underneath it.

A spring is a slope, so the load climbs as it stretches. Which means the number
any person meets is decided by the body doing the moving, not by the color you
hung on the carriage. A taller client travels farther and finishes higher up the
climb. A shorter client finishes lower down the same line. Same setting, two
different exercises, and now you can say why out loud.

This is happening in every exercise you teach, not just footwork. Anywhere a
spring stretches, the body in front of you decides how far.

There is a second thing about a body that changes the number, and it is the one
almost every teacher has backwards. It is body weight, and it does not work the
way teacher training told you it does. That one is a bigger conversation and I
want to give it the room it deserves.

Next time I want to tell you how a Pilates teacher ended up doing math on
purpose. It was not a plan.

Kaleen

## Version B: "Between their body and the reformer"

Built around a real student's words. Explains the model and lands on being able
to narrate it to the client, which is the outcome students report most often.
Warmest, and the strongest on Nikki's "make sense of their experience" brief.

**Subject line options**
1. What is happening between their body and the machine
2. The language you were never given
3. Stop guessing, start explaining

**Preview text:** Your clients have been asking you a question you could not answer.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

A student told me what changed for her after all this, and she said it better
than I could:

> "It provided me with more understanding and language to help myself and future
> students understand what is happening between their body and the reformer."

Between their body and the reformer. That is the thing nobody hands you in
training. You get the settings, and you get the cues, and the space in between,
where the actual work happens, is left for you to guess at.

So here is what is in that space.

A spring is a slope. The load climbs as it stretches. Which means the load is not
a property of the spring, it is a property of the meeting between that spring and
that body. Move the same spring a longer distance and it is a heavier exercise.
Move it a shorter distance and it is a lighter one.

Your clients are not all moving it the same distance. They cannot be. They are
different heights with different limb lengths, and the carriage travels as far as
their legs make it travel.

That is why the tall one cruises and the small one struggles on the setting you
gave them both. It is not effort, or focus, or whether your cue landed. It is
distance, and distance is set by the body.

Once you can see that, two things change.

You can tell a client what they are feeling and why, which is a very different
conversation from telling them they are doing it wrong. And when they say they
cannot feel it, you have somewhere to go. You can change how far they travel, or
change what they are traveling against, and you can say out loud why you are
doing it.

That is the difference between guessing and teaching.

There is a second thing about a body that changes the load, and most teachers
have it exactly backwards. It is body weight. What you were taught about it is
not quite right, and it deserves its own conversation rather than a paragraph
here.

Next time, how I ended up here, which is a less flattering story than you might
expect.

Kaleen

## Version C: "It was never the spring"

Declarative, in the style of the earlier version C drafts. Reframes the spring as
one input and the body as the deciding one. Most quotable, least instructional.

**Subject line options**
1. It was never really about the spring
2. The body decides, not the spring
3. Your client is the variable you were never given

**Preview text:** The spring sets a slope. The body decides where you land on it.

---

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }},

Three emails in, here is the thing I most want you to keep.

The spring is not deciding what your client feels. Your client is.

The spring sets a slope, and then it is finished having opinions. It gets heavier
the further it is pulled, and that is the entire contribution it makes. What
decides the number a person actually meets is how far that person pulls it.

And that is a fact about the body, not the equipment.

A taller client pushes the carriage farther, so they finish higher up the climb.
A shorter client finishes lower down the same line. You gave them the same
setting and they did two different exercises, and neither of them did anything
wrong.

We have been arguing about red versus blue for decades while the actual variable
walked into the studio and lay down.

I want to be precise, because this is the part that matters in the room. Knowing
this does not make you faster at picking springs. It makes you able to say a true
thing out loud. When a client tells you they cannot feel it, you can explain what
is happening between their body and the machine, in ordinary language, and then
you can change it on purpose instead of cycling through cues and hoping.

That is the whole return on understanding this. Not arithmetic. Confidence, and
something honest to say.

There is a second thing about a body that changes the load, and it is the one I
see teachers get backwards most often. It is body weight. Nearly everyone was
taught a version of it that does not hold up, and it is a bigger conversation
than I can do justice to here.

Next time, the part where I explain how I ended up doing math about Pilates, and
what it cost me to learn it the slow way.

Kaleen

## First pass, retired

The original three drafts aimed at equipment settings, with "you can know the
load before class" as the payoff. Retired for two reasons, both worth remembering
when drafting email 5 and the sales sequence.

1. **Wrong product.** The model pointed at gear position, rope length, footbar
   height, and carriage position. That is an apparatus answer, and it teed up a
   workshop about adjustments. The next thing the reader is sold is a mini course
   about body weight and height. The nurture sequence has to land where the cart
   opens.
2. **Wrong payoff.** "You can predict this in advance" is not what teachers want.
   What they want is to explain to a client what they are feeling and why, and to
   know what to change when a client says they cannot feel it. Foresight is a
   planning benefit. Confidence in the moment is the real one.

The retired framing is still useful material, but it belongs to Pilates Physics
101, where working through each adjustment is the actual curriculum.
