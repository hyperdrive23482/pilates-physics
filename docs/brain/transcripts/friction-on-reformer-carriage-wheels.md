# Friction on reformer carriage wheels
Is friction a big deal when it comes to how hard a reformer Pilates exercise is? Hi, I'm Kaen. I'm a mechanical engineer and I teach Pilates physics. Let's look at footwork on a contemporary machine that has wheels with bearings. The force of friction always works opposite from the direction of movement. So, if we're pressing out, the force of friction is pointing this way. The force of friction at the wheels or the rolling resistance is calculated by adding the force of friction at the bearings and the force of friction at the wheel where it meets the aluminum rail together. We have standard values for all these components. So we can calculate that 150lb person on a 50 lb carriage experiences a friction force of about 7.8 lb. And what about a heavier person say 300 lb on the same carriage? Well, their force of friction is about 13.7 lb. Now, we don't really care about the absolute values here. We care about the relative values. So, the difference between them, and that's 5.9 lb. Should I use the force of friction to determine which settings to use for my students in footwork? No. The force of the springs in footwork goes from about 30 lbs to about 110 lbs, depending, of course, on your spring settings and how far they stretch. Five or six pounds is not a huge factor in determining which settings to use. In fact, I would choose to look at a student's height, so how far they stretch a spring and their own strength capability before I ever looked at the force of friction here. But for an exercise that uses a really, really light spring, like single arm work facing sideways, you may have experienced that awkward moment where the carriage goes out, but it doesn't really want to come back in. And that can be explained by the rolling resistance. Look at this spring chart. I mean, that spring doesn't hit 7.8 8 lb of force, which is the rolling resistance, until about 12 in of extension. So, if you're working with the carriage close to home and the spring not stretched very much, you might have to actively pull the carriage home. Now, what about classical wheels? If we do the same calculations for a wheel that's slightly bigger, uses a steel bushing, and has a harder material that contacts the aluminum, a 150lb person on a 50 lb carriage experiences about 4.4 lb of force due to friction. and a 300lb person would experience about 7.6 lb. So, how can that be less? Well, classical wheels have a lower coefficient of friction between the wheel and the rail and a higher coefficient of friction between the bushing and the wheel. So, they sort of have a little trade-off there. And of course, the absolute values could vary a little bit based on the manufacturers of both sets of wheels I'm using in this example. And of course, poorly maintained equipment can increase the rolling resistance. If you've got hair or dust or dirt or you don't grease your classical bushings, that force of friction is going to go up. So, will I start adding rolling resistance to my loading scenarios? No. But unless you are fighting the carriage not returning on a very very light spring, it's not something that needs to be considered to make directionally sound teaching decisions.
