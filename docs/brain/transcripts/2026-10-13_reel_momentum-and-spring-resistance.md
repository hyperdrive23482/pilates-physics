# Momentum and Spring Resistance (Reel, 2026-10-13)
**Format:** Reel · **Posted:** 2026-10-13
**Topic:** Whiteboard walkthrough of whether momentum steals spring resistance in footwork. Spring force is identical at any speed, but a fast repetition lets the muscles push off harder early and ride the springs at the top, so peak muscle force can drop from about 88 lb toward the spring average (about 57 lb). Not bad, just a different intention: power training.
**Key concepts:** momentum, spring resistance, force vs. extension graph, footwork, Balanced Body springs, red red green, muscle force, area between curves, carriage acceleration, power training, intention

## Spoken

Is momentum stealing your spring resistance? Let's look at the basic physics. So plot footwork over an 18 inch repetition. We start at zero inches of extension, we go up to 18, we come back down to zero. Extension is on the bottom. The vertical axis is force. Now let's assume that we're using Balanced Body springs in footwork, two reds and a green. That means we're going to start down at about 26 pounds, go up to about 88 pounds, and then come back down to 26 pounds.

Throughout this repetition, the muscles and the springs are trading energy. And so if we plot the muscle force on this graph, the lines are going to be very similar, right? So the muscles are going to start a little bit more, they're going to cross over and peak just under 88 pounds, and then they'll come back down the same way, cross about the same length, and return. The area between these two lines here, here, that represents the carriage speeding up and slowing down. So in this example it's very small. It's very small speed. That means they're going pretty slow.

Now let's look at a fast repetition where we've got more momentum. So we'll graph it the same way, with force at the top, 0, 18, 0. The spring resistance looks exactly the same, 26 to 88, back down to 26. What's different here is the muscles. So when we use momentum, that means we're pushing off a little harder, and so the force the muscles output is a little higher at the beginning, and it's going to only rise a little bit and then it's going to come back down, right? So you're riding the springs up here at the top a little bit more. Now when we look at the area between the curves, you can see that the carriage is speeding up and slowing down more. That's how we know this is going faster.

And the thing we all care about, which is why we're watching this reel, is the force. So we know our slow one peaks around 88 pounds. It almost gets to the maximum spring force. Down here, when we're going fast, the muscles peak lower. You could actually go as low as the average between the minimum and maximum spring force. So in this case, you might only have to exert 57-ish pounds to get the carriage to stretch the same distance.

Now, is this bad? No. Context matters. We mostly teach Pilates up here in the slower range, but this movement is actually power training. It has a real purpose. This is why it's so important to understand the intention of what you're teaching. Sometimes you might want slow, sometimes you might want fast. Understand the trade-offs so you know that your teaching is actually going in the direction you want it to.
