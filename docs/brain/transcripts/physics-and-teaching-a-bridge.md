# Physics and Teaching a Bridge
**Topic:** How Kaleen applies bridge physics in group classes — using supportive spring behavior to adjust on the fly, foot bar/gear bar baseline carried over from footwork, layered progressions, and her actual in-class spring-options script

Last time I talked about the physics of a bridge on a reformer, but what good is physics if it doesn't help you change how you teach for the better? Today I'm talking about how physics has informed how I teach a bridge on a reformer. There are three major components to this exercise. Spring behavior, body weight, and joint angles. Here's how each of those impact how I teach. First, we know that the spring behavior is supportive. That means more springs makes the exercise easier. So, I know that when I'm offering my students spring options or I'm making a change on the fly, that adding a spring is going to help a student who is struggling. Second, body weight has an impact on how hard the exercise is because the portion of your body that you have to lift in the air presses the carriage down and out. This means that when I offer spring options at the beginning of the exercise, I might mention this as something for my students to consider when they pick their springs. Third, joint angles. I have my students adjust their foot bar and gear bar when they're doing footwork. So, this is where I try and keep their reformer settings for the rest of the class. So, they're coming into the bridge with a baseline adjustment for their body. However, I know this doesn't always work. So, if adjusting the springs isn't enough to get them experiencing the intent of the exercise, I know that I can change the foot bar height or even the gear bar position to get them into a more comfortable and functional position. Here are three examples of how this might show up practically in my classes. If I have a very deconditioned client who has back pain, they may choose to do their bridge with their feet on the standing platform. My very tall and heavy client may need the heaviest springs with the foot bar lower one slot and the gear bar out. Or to progress my students, I might layer in variations and encourage them to stop at the one where they feel maxed out. First, we bridge. Then, we hold at the top. Then, we weight shift, making one leg do more work than the other. And then, we might even lift one leg off the foot bar. In my classes, I prioritize time spent moving. So, I don't start an exercise off by asking a bunch of questions, explaining all the theory, and giving each individual student their own settings. I take 10 seconds to explain the intent and the spring options, and then get them moving, and then I'll make adjustments on the fly. Here's my script. Change your springs for bridging. Your job is to keep the carriage closed as you lift your hips. You may feel this most in your hamstrings. The most supportive spring options are two reds and a green. And you might choose this if you cramp a lot. Baseline spring settings are two reds and a blue, and hard is two reds. Physics tells us that our students body shape, size, and strength impacts how they experience the exercise.
