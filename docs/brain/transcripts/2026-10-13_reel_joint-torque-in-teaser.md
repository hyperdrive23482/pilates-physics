# Joint Torque in Teaser (Reel, 2026-10-13)
**Format:** Reel · **Posted:** 2026-10-13
**Topic:** Why some clients nail Teaser the first time and others struggle. Beyond strength, leg weight and leg length change the torque at the hip (torque = force x moment arm), with worked numbers comparing clients of different weight and different height.
**Key concepts:** joint torque, Teaser, internal vs. external torque, moment arm, tau = F x r, hip, leg weight, limb length, body weight, client height, adapting and progressing exercises

## Spoken

Why do some clients nail Teaser the first time and others struggle? You've probably seen this in your class. You've got two new people, and one can pop right up into Teaser with legs extended, and the other one really struggles. Their knees bend, they can't quite fold themselves in half or extend their legs. Maybe they tip backwards.

Of course, this has a lot to do with the client's strength. But does it have everything to do with their strength? Not at all. Each person who comes into your class is built differently, and the weight and length of their limbs significantly impacts the forces that their body feels. Let's walk through a couple examples with Teaser. Let's look at it through the lens of joint torque.

Torque itself is a rotational force. When applied, it spins or twists an object around a point. All of our joints create torque with the muscles that cross them. The muscles cause the joints to pivot. Therefore, the muscles are creating joint torque. That's an internal torque. External loads, like springs or the weight of certain body segments, act on the joint to create external torque. Torque, whether internal or external, is represented by the equation tau equals F times r, where F is the force and r is the perpendicular distance from the point to the force, aka the moment arm.

So we don't do math while we're teaching. Why do we care about an equation? Just looking at this equation tells you which two components impact the amount of torque either applied or exerted. Because of that multiplication sign between the force and the moment arm, we know that if one of those increases, the overall torque will also increase.

Let's apply some example numbers for two scenarios. First, we'll look at two people who are the same height but different weights. We can see here the 130-pound person and the 250-pound person have the same leg lengths, so if their legs are at 45 degrees, the moment arm is going to be the same. But their leg weights, that segment of one leg, is different. Therefore, the torque that they experience at their hip joint is significantly different. This is a 92% increase from the lighter person to the heavier person.

If we looked at two people who were the same weight but different heights, we would see something similar, although not quite as drastic. So these two people, 5 foot 2 and 6 foot tall, both weigh 160 pounds. You can see that their leg weight stays the same, but the moment arm changes. So there is a slight increase in torque experienced by the tall person.

Now, of course, in real life, limb dimensions, weight distribution, strength levels, all of those things vary. And you don't need to do math for every single one of your clients to be able to adapt for them. I think what's interesting in these examples is the relative difference, because when you understand the things that impact how hard an exercise is for the individuals that come into your class, you can better adapt and progress every human that walks in the door.
