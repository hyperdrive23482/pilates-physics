# Intro to chair physics
**Topic:** Why chair springs apply force at varying angles based on pedal height; spring stretch vs. pedal height is non-linear; vertical vs. horizontal force components; lower pedal = heavier load

Let's talk about the physics of the Pilates chair. We are about to level up our Pilates physics skills, y'all. You ready? Unlike a reformer where the springs apply force in the direction of the carriage movement, always on a chair, the springs apply force in different angles based on the pedal height. Because there are so many different kinds of chairs with different dimensions like petal length, petal height, height to the top of the chair, where the pivot point is, we are going to use the chair that I have, which is an older balance body combo chair. Look at some numbers and then try and create some principles that we can apply to other situations or other chairs. To be a great Pilates teacher, you don't need to know the exact numbers for every piece of equipment you're working on. You need to know concepts. So that's what we're looking at today. So, the first concept we need to recognize is that with a chair, we typically care most about how high the pedal is from the floor. But because it's an arm on a pivot point, as the pedal rises, it also gets closer to the front or the back of the chair. All that means is that if we change the height of the pedal by one inch, it doesn't necessarily mean the spring changes by one inch. With my particular chair, you can see that the relationship between spring stretch and pedal height from the floor up here is not linear. But if you compare that to reformer carriage travel versus spring stretch, you can see it's linear down here. Now, the other thing you need to remember is that for every force that's at an angle, it has a horizontal component and a vertical component. You may recognize this from some of my videos on rope angle on a reformer. Here's an example. On my chair, if I push the pedal halfway down, I'm stretching the two black springs about 6.4 in. That means the spring force is about 69.5 lb at 29° below horizontal. So the vertical component of that force is 33.7 lb and the horizontal component of that force is 60.8 lb. So why do we care? Well, we care because we want to know the vertical component of the force for this exercise. I weigh 180 lb. So downward force on the pedal is 180 lb. But the pedal force going upward is 33.7 pounds. So when the pedal is halfway down, at that point in time, I'm controlling 146.3 pounds over the whole pedal height from floor to top. The black line is the actual spring force, how far it's stretched, but it's always at a different angle. Then the purple line is actually the vertical component of the force at each of those pedal heights. Now, do you need to memorize this? No. Your basic takeaway is that the lower the pedal is to the floor, the heavier it is. And in my next videos, I'll look at how this concept applies to different body weights, different exercises, different body limb lengths, and also different spring Planes.
