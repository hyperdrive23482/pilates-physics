# Physics of a Plank on a Reformer
**Topic:** How body angle affects weight distribution and horizontal force in planks; supportive vs. resistive spring roles; progression strategies based on intent

Let's talk physics of a plank on a reformer. Planks on a reformer are a great example of an exercise where body weight and spring tension affect how difficult the exercise is, but maybe not for the reasons you might think. Let's take a look at three examples. First, a high plank with hands on the foot bar. In this position, the body angle compared to horizontal is pretty steep. So the lower body is taking a lot of the body weight and the horizontal force pushing the carriage out is quite big. Then there's a medium plank. Here the angle is a little less steep compared to the high plank, but the lower body is still taking a little bit more weight. So there's still a significant push horizontally of the carriage outward. It's less than the high plank, but it's more than the next variation. In a low plank with your hands on the floor and feet on the carriage, your body angle is nearly horizontal. So, it is the most balanced upper body to lower body weight distribution you're going to get. And the horizontal force pushing the carriage out is the least. So, what does this mean for your teaching? Well, in terms of setup, in a high plank, for example, someone with a bigger body weight is going to need a little bit more support than someone in a lower body weight because they have more force pushing the carriage out. That relative difference between body weights is going to decrease a little bit in the lower plank versions. In terms of progressing this exercise, I think about it in three ways, and they all depend on your intent. So let's look at the first two with a high plank. So say you want the intent of the exercise to be to keep the carriage closed, maybe more core focused. In that case, the springs play a supporting role. So you would remove spring support to make the challenge harder. On the flip side, if your intent is to load the shoulders a little bit more, the springs now play a resistive role. So you would increase the springs to increase the challenge because that makes pushing the carriage out harder. Or finally you could change the load of the shoulders. You could progress the intensity of the shoulder work by instead of changing springs going from a high plank to a medium plank and then to a low plank. I love this exercise example in terms of progression because it really hammers home the point of you need to know three things when you're considering teaching and progressing. First, you need to know the intention of your exercise. Second, you need to know how the piece of equipment that you're teaching on delivers load. And third, you need to know what your client is telling you, both verbally and non-verbally.
