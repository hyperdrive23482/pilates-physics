# Bridge Press Out
**Topic:** Why pressing the carriage out in a bridge doesn't feel harder — torque on the knee increases as the shin levels out, but Hooke's Law makes the springs heavier with extension, and those effects roughly cancel; tested with a 20 lb kettlebell which exposed what happens without that scaling

In my last video on the physics of a bridge on a reformer, I explained that the more horizontal your shin angle is, the harder it is for your hamstrings. It's more torque on the knee. But if you're doing a bridge on a reformer and you press the carriage out, why doesn't it feel harder? The first concept we need to cover is torque on the knee. So when you're bridging with your feet on the foot bar, your shin tends not to be vertical. It tends to be at an angle. And because of that, we're putting more torque on the knee because there is a perpendicular distance between your heels on the bar and your knee joint. So the more your knee angle levels out, the higher the torque on your knee joint. If we compare these two versions of a bridge, you can see that the shin angle on one is steeper than the other. And because the equation for torque is force times distance, we know the force is the same. Distance changes. And because it the distance gets bigger, the more horizontal the shin is, the torque gets bigger. Okay, if that's true, then why doesn't the bridge feel way harder as you press the carriage out? Well, it's because of spring physics. Remember the spring behavior in a bridge is supportive? That means more springs make the exercise easier. And we know because of Hook's law that the more you stretch the springs, the heavier they get. So the further you move the carriage out, not only is the torque on your knee increasing, but you're also increasing the spring resistance, which gives you more support. So those two things kind of balance each other out. Cool, right? I have felt this phenomenon personally because I built a weight stack for my reformer so I could swap out my springs and use kettle bells for resistance. And let me tell you, when I put a 20 pound kettle bell on there, it feels exactly the same as springs when the carriage is closed. But when I press the carriage out, I about fall off because the load does not scale with the carriage movement, and my hamstrings are just not strong enough. Oh. Uh-oh. I'm stuck. Sometimes in Pilates, I'm annoyed that the beginning of an exercise is lighter than the end of an exercise. Why can't it just be hard throughout the whole range? And then there are other exercises like this one where I think it's pretty cool. The linear relationship between spring stretch and resistance makes it so that the springs support you throughout a range of motion in a way that a weight stack can't.
