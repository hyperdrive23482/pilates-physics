# Carousel: The physics of momentum

## Slide 1 (cover)

**Momentum is *stealing* your spring resistance.**

Whether that's bad or good depends on your intent.

Graphic (optional, bottom of slide): `cover-line-art`

## Slide 2

**Slow down. It's *harder*.**

Someone moves so fast during arms in straps that the ropes go slack and the
spring resistance disappears.

You ask them to slow down, and they tell you it's harder. They're right. Here's
the physics of why.

## Slide 3

**Mass in *motion***

Graphic: `equation` (p = m × v, labeled momentum, mass, velocity)

Momentum tells you how hard it is to stop something that's moving. Change the
mass or the velocity, and the momentum changes with it.

## Slide 4

**Same carriage, two *different* rides**

Footwork: you feel the springs the whole way. Heavier springs, slower pace.

Jumpboard: push, coast, catch. Lighter springs, a fast push at the start, then a
coast until you catch the carriage coming home.

Jumping uses lighter springs, and somehow you feel more burn and more
breathlessness.

## Slide 5

**A spring is *not* one weight**

Graphic: `fig-01-spring-force`

Footwork on red, red and green, 18 inches of stretch. The springs pull 26 lbs at
home and 88 lbs at full extension.

Springs don't care about speed. They only care how far they're stretched.

## Slide 6

**Slow: your muscles *track* the springs**

Graphic: `fig-02-slow-rep`

Muscle force stays close to spring force the whole way. A little more to get the
carriage moving, a little less as it slows near the end. Peak muscle force: about
83 lbs.

## Slide 7

**The gap is the carriage *changing speed***

Graphic: `fig-03-slow-gap`

The space between the curves is the force that speeds the carriage up or slows it
down. On a slow rep it stays small, so your muscles carry nearly the full spring
load.

## Slide 8

**Fast: same springs, *lower* peak**

Graphic: `fig-04-fast-rep`

To move the carriage faster, the gap has to grow. Momentum carries it through the
heavy end of the stretch, so your muscles never climb as high. Peak: 57 lbs, down
from 83.

## Slide 9

**57 is *halfway* between 26 and 88**

Graphic: `number-line-26-57-88`

At this pace, muscle force barely changes while the springs do all the rising and
falling. It's the lowest peak your muscles can get away with on these springs.

Go faster and the effort becomes a hard push, then a coast. Sound familiar?
That's jumping.

## Slide 10

**Not bad. Just *different*.**

Goal: peak force. Go slow. Muscle peak 83 lbs.

Goal: power. Go fast. Muscle peak 57 lbs.

Each probably calls for different spring weights. Knowing which one you're after
is what makes progressive overload possible.

## Slide 11

**Learn it slow, *add* speed later**

When a movement is new, the nervous system stiffens the joints by contracting
opposing muscles at the same time. That is co-contraction.

As the movement is learned, co-contraction drops and the muscles work more
efficiently. That's one more reason to teach new movements slowly.

Osu et al., J Neurophysiol, 2002

## Slide 12 (close)

**Speed is a choice. Make it *on purpose*.**

Momentum isn't cheating. It trains something different.

The full post, with the math behind these graphs, is at the link in bio. New to
spring physics? Start with Springs 101.

## Caption

You've given the correction a hundred times. Slow down. And every time, your
client tells you it's harder.

Here's why. On a fast rep, momentum carries the carriage through the heavy end of
the stretch, so your muscles never have to meet the springs' full pull. Same
springs, same 18 inches, and the peak muscle force drops from 83 lbs to 57.

That doesn't make fast reps wrong. Slow trains peak force. Fast trains power. The
question is which one you're after today.
