# Carousel: Personalization in Pilates

## Slide 1 (cover)

**Everyone said personalization. Nobody said how.**

At the Pilates Journal Expo, panel after panel landed on the same word.
Personalization is what makes a studio endure. Not one person disagreed, and
then the conversation moved on, because nobody asked the next question.

## Slide 2

**The floor is not the ceiling**

Right now, personalization mostly means you remember that your nine o'clock
client had a kid with a basketball game last weekend, and you ask how it went.
That matters. It is the reason someone drives past two other studios to get to
yours. It is also the part our industry has already solved.

## Slide 3

**They want their bodies seen, not just their weekends**

When the relationship is the only thing we personalize, we tell clients
something we do not mean. That the expertise is not really the product. That
they could get the same hour from anyone warm. People come to us to learn about
their bodies, to move them, to change them.

## Slide 4

**Personalization is two problems, not one**

It is not a single thing we are all failing at. It is two different problems
that happen to share a name, and they need completely different solutions. The
first one lives inside the hour. The second one lives between hours. Only one
of them is teachable.

## Slide 5

**Programming that layers instead of resetting**

Most group classes are built as standalone events. Everyone arrives, everyone
does the thing on the same settings, and next week starts over from zero. A
class that personalizes is a step in a sequence, where the same exercise offers
two or three honest entry points, chosen on purpose rather than memorized. None
of them is the lesser one.

## Slide 6

**Two people on the same spring are not doing the same exercise**

Limb length, torso length, where someone's weight actually sits, all of it
interacts with the reformer to create a load that belongs to that body alone. A
spring is not one weight to begin with. Once you can see what the load is really
doing in that position on that body, you can adapt for someone you have never
taught before.

## Slide 7

**The good news about the first problem**

It is a skill, which means it is learnable. It comes from understanding what the
load is doing rather than memorizing which setting goes with which exercise.
Nobody handed you that in your training, and it was never your job to invent it
on your own.

## Slide 8

**The thread you cannot hold**

Everything that happens in the room happens in the moment and stays there. The
setting or adaptation you found in March is gone by May, and it never becomes
evidence of progress. This half is not a skill problem. In the moment you are
teaching, not recording, and you should not have to be.

## Slide 9

**Your client never gets to see what changed**

A runner gets a pace graph. Someone who lifts gets reps, sets, and load over
time. Both are written down, trending in a direction they can see. Pilates gives
them a good feeling and a see you next week, and on the month they are looking at
their bank statement deciding what to cut, a good feeling argues for nothing.

## Slide 10

**Replace, or remember**

Technology that replaces the expert does separate us from people. Technology that
remembers for the expert does the opposite. Ambient charting tools saved 7,260
physicians more than 15,000 hours of documentation in a single year, and nearly
half of their patients said their doctor spent less time looking at a computer.
It did not come between them, it gave back eye contact.

## Slide 11 (close)

**Learn to see it, then keep the thread**

Learn to see what the load is doing and you can adapt for anyone who walks into
the room. Keep the thread across sessions and you can show someone what a year
with you actually did. The first one you solve by learning. The second one you
solve by having a system, and almost none of us have one.

---

## Caption

Every leader on that stage agreed that personalization is what makes a studio
endure. Nobody said what it means.

It turns out to be two problems. Adapting to the body in front of you, which is
a skill you can learn. And holding the thread across sessions, which no amount of
study fixes, because in the moment you are teaching, not writing things down.

I am not asking anyone to turn Pilates into a metric. I am asking that what
already happened in the room stop evaporating the second the session ends.

Full post at the link in bio.

Which of the two is harder in your week, adapting in the room or remembering what
you did last time?

## Design notes

- Dark ground (`--bg #0e0d0b`), cream serif type, one accent only
  (`--accent #f09f26`). See docs/design-system.md.
- One italic accent phrase per slide title at most, never two. Suggested accents:
  slide 1 "how", slide 2 "floor", slide 4 "two problems", slide 8 "thread",
  slide 10 "remember", slide 11 "keep the thread".
- Slides 5 and 6 are the pair that carries the teaching. If you only illustrate
  two slides, illustrate those: a layered class diagram for 5, the same spring
  setting on two different bodies for 6.
- Slide 9 wants a visual. A pace graph and a reps-over-time chart on the left, an
  empty frame on the right, is the whole argument without a word of copy.
- Keep the middle slides plain so the copy carries. Grid paper texture and corner
  registration crosses on the cover and close only.
