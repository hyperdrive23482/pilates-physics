## Slides

1. Spooky ways to change spring tension without changing springs. [visual: Kaleen with ghosts, bat, cobweb, "BOO!"]
2. Most adjustments on the reformer impact spring resistance. That's because they change how far the spring is stretched. The more a spring is stretched, the heavier it is. The less a spring is stretched, the lighter it is.
3. 1) Rope Length. How long the ropes are when using them changes the spring resistance. Longer ropes: lighter. Shorter ropes: heavier.
4. 2) Rope Grip Position. Similar to rope length, where you hold the ropes changes spring resistance when using them. Long loops: lightest. Short loops: medium. Choked up: heaviest.
5. 3) Footbar height. When using a footbar that's on a pivot, raising or lowering the footbar changes the distance the footbar is to the carriage. Lower: lighter. Higher: heavier.
6. 4) Body distance to shoulder rests. When using the ropes, such as supine arms in straps, seated arm work, or rowing. Closer to ropes: lighter. Further from ropes: heavier.
7. 5) Gear position. When using the footbar or ropes. Gear 1: heaviest. Gear 2: medium. Gear 3: lightest.
8. Whether or not you mean to, it changes. When adjusting to accommodate different limb lengths, joint mobility, or available range under tension, be aware that your adjustment can inadvertently change the spring resistance the client feels. Sometimes it's okay, and sometimes it's no longer supporting your teaching intent.

## Caption

You didn't touch the springs. So why does it feel different? 👻

Springs get heavier the further they stretch. Almost every adjustment on the reformer changes how far that is: rope length, where you hold the ropes, footbar height, how far the body sits from the shoulder rests, and gear position.

Most of the time we make these changes for good reasons, like longer legs or a client who needs more range. The spring load changes as a result, whether you meant it to or not.

Sometimes that's fine. Sometimes it quietly works against what you were trying to teach.
